*The Sun · distance, motion & the analemma*

*Figure caption:* **Globe.** Both panels are what a level camera aimed at the year's mean Sun would frame. The 8 is one rigid curve on the celestial sphere, the same for every observer at every hour, so its drawn shape never changes — slide φ and it only turns with you.

*Figure caption:* **Flat disc, steel-manned.** The Sun circles above the disc at exactly the height that makes the equinox noon altitude correct for *your* latitude — the model is given its best free parameter, not a straw one.

Move to mid-latitudes and it breaks. Solstice altitudes miss by 10–16° at φ = 45–60°, and — unlike the globe's rigid celestial curve — **the flat 8's very shape morphs as you travel**, from 65°×16° at the equator down to 29°×11° by φ = 60°. Slide φ and watch it deform: no analemma photograph has ever shown that.

And the Sun's altitude never reaches zero. On a flat disc **the horizon can never cut the 8** — no sunset at any latitude, and no polar night at any pole, where six months of measured darkness are the plainest fact of Arctic life. The Sun can only shrink into the distance; it measurably does not.

The Sun's distance was pinned down by several completely independent routes — triangulating a planet from two points on Earth, timing Venus crossing the Sun's face from opposite hemispheres, and, most elegantly, using light itself as a ruler. Then, once a year of noon Suns is photographed from one spot, the sky draws a figure-8 — the **analemma** — whose exact shape is a fingerprint of the geometry producing it.

The routes share no assumptions: parallax needs only a baseline on Earth and simultaneous angles; the transit method needs timing; the light-time method needs a stopwatch and a terrestrial measurement of *c*. They agree to a fraction of a percent. One number, many roads.

## Rømer's clock, run in reverse [evidence: prediction]

In 1676 at the Paris Observatory, Ole Rømer noticed that the eclipses of Jupiter's moon **Io** — a natural clock ticking every 42.5 h — ran *late* when Earth was far from Jupiter and *early* when near, and he predicted the 9 November eclipse would arrive 10 minutes behind Cassini's tables[1]. It did. Light, he concluded, takes time to travel: about 22 minutes (his estimate) across Earth's orbit. Huygens turned that into the first speed of light.

**Now run it backwards.** By 1849 Fizeau had measured *c* on the ground — a toothed wheel and a mirror 8.6 km apart in Paris, no astronomy involved — and Foucault refined it to ~298,000 km/s[2]. Feed that *c* into Io's timings: the eclipse delay swings by **≈16.6 minutes** between Jupiter's opposition and conjunction, and the only distance that swing can correspond to is the *diameter of Earth's orbit*: 2 AU = c × ~998 s ⇒ **1 AU ≈ 149.6 million km**. Note what is *not* assumed: Jupiter's distance comes free from its 11.86-year period via Kepler's third law, which needs only angles; the relative map of the solar system was already known — Io's lag simply sets its absolute scale[3].

*Figure caption:* **Left:** the Earth and Jupiter round the Sun, to scale in astronomical units (orbits drawn circular and coplanar: the real eccentricities move the swing by a few percent). The dashes are the light leaving Jupiter for the Earth, with its time of flight. In the medallion, Jupiter up close and to its own scale: its shadow points away from the Sun, and Io (enlarged, and slowed down about thirty times) goes into it once a turn. **Right:** the light time over the last two years, one dot per few eclipses seen; it swings by about 16.6 minutes every 399 days.

$$\Delta t = \frac{D}{c} \implies 2\,\text{au} = c\cdot\Delta t_{\text{max}} \approx 299,792\ \text{km}/\text{s}\times 998\ \text{s}$$

The flat model has no account of this at all: a lamp a few thousand kilometres up gives Earth–Sun and Earth–Jupiter distance variations of a few thousand km — light-time wobbles of *hundredths of a second*, not sixteen minutes ticked out by a moon on schedule.

**Three centuries of eclipses, in one file.** J. H. Lieske gathered 16,802 timings of eclipses of Jupiter's four large moons, from the manuscripts Delisle collected in Paris and Pingré's annals of the seventeenth century — Delambre's collection, long thought lost — to the observatories' publications of 1983[9][10]. 8,822 of them are of Io, from 401 observatories. Each line is one time: the instant Io faded into Jupiter's shadow (a disappearance) or came out of it (a reappearance), mostly by eye, and at Harvard from 1878 to 1903 with a photometer (439 timings).

**What is fitted, era by era.** Io's clock (its rate and a slow drift); where the shadow points, which is where the Sun is seen from Jupiter (an angle); the half-width of the shadow, which Io crosses off its centre as Jupiter's seasons turn; the 437.6-day wobble that Europa forces on Io's orbit[13]; one offset per observatory; and one more number: how many seconds the light takes per astronomical unit of Earth–Jupiter distance, A/c. The distances are in astronomical units only. The orbits are angles[11], and Jupiter's distance from the Sun, 5.203 AU, comes from Kepler's third law and the two periods, 11.86 years against one. Kilometres enter once, at the very end, through the velocity of light measured in laboratories[2]. Times are turned into the uniform time of the orbits with ΔT[12].

$$t_{\text{obs}} = t_{J} + \frac{A}{c}\,d$$
**A/c = 502.7 ± 1.7 s** ⇒ **A = 150.7 ± 0.5 million km**

t_obs is the time written in the logbook, t_J the instant Io crossed the shadow's edge, d the Earth–Jupiter distance in AU, A the astronomical unit and c the velocity of light. The figure: the eclipses' delay, averaged by distance in each of the six eras (one colour each), against the distance. Every era's points lie on one line, whose slope is A/c: about 8.4 minutes per astronomical unit. Its zero is not measured (Io's clock absorbs a constant): only the rise is.

**The six eras together:** A/c = 502.7 ± 1.7 s, so **A = 150.7 ± 0.5 million km**. The adopted value, 149.6 million km[4], is 499.0 s: ours is 0.7 % long, 2.2 times its error. One fit of all 8,675 eclipses at once gives 504.6 ± 1.6 s; Harvard's photometry alone, 492.6 ± 3.0 s (-1.3 %). Europa's wobble comes out at 199–212 s in every era, 3.4 minutes.

**Limits.** The eras disagree more than their errors allow (χ² = 9.3 for 5 degrees of freedom; 497–508 s), so the error of the mean is widened by the Birge ratio[16]. The orbits are approximate elements, wrong on Jupiter by a few hundredths of a degree with the period of its year; a free term of Jupiter's year takes that up, so only the 399-day swing measures the light time. Without that term the eras range over 496–526 s; with the shapes of the orbits of JPL's DE440[14] instead (a check: DE440 rests in part on radar ranging), 497–510 s. Most timings are by eye, and a small telescope loses a fainter Io a little earlier: a drift with distance, of opposite sign for disappearances and reappearances, is fitted. Lieske reduced the local times to universal time and corrected the longitudes of some observatories from the residuals of an ephemeris[10]; a longitude moves every timing of an observatory by the same amount, and each observatory has its own free offset, so no such correction can reach the light time. 147 eclipses more than three standard deviations from the fit of the others are left out.

> **What would have vindicated the flat model:** Io's clock alone fitting the eclipses as well as the light time does, or a delay that does not follow the Earth–Jupiter distance. Io's clock alone leaves 210 s of scatter; with the light time, 52.9 s.

## The flat model's best curve for Io's delay

**A flat Earth has no distance to Jupiter that changes by two astronomical units in a year,** so nothing in it predicts a delay. Give it the best chance anyway: let Io's clock carry a free curve that repeats with Jupiter's synodic cycle, two harmonics, four free numbers, its phase φ read from Jupiter's place against the Sun in the sky; and a free term of Jupiter's 11.86-year year, two more, the same as the globe was given. Six free numbers where the globe has one.

$${\delta(\varphi) = a_1\cos\varphi + b_1\sin\varphi + a_2\cos 2\varphi + b_2\sin 2\varphi}$$
$${\delta = \frac{A}{c}\sqrt{a_J^2 + 1 - 2a_J\cos\varphi}}$$

φ is the angle between the Earth and Jupiter seen from the Sun (0 at opposition, when Jupiter is opposite the Sun in the sky), a₁, b₁, a₂, b₂ the flat curve's free numbers, a_J = 5.203 AU. The figure: the flat curve fitted to the 330 years (dashed) over the curve one astronomical unit predicts (thick), and Io's clock alone (the flat line). The two curves lie on top of each other.

**The flat curve's free numbers (as a phase of Io's clock: a delay counts negative), and what one astronomical unit predicts for them**

| term | flat model, fitted | one AU predicts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a₁ | 493.5 ± 2.0 s | 497.2 s |

**The free curve fits exactly as well as the globe** (rms 52.8 s against 52.9 s, with three more free numbers), **and its numbers fall where one astronomical unit puts them:** a swing of 493.5 ± 2.0 s against 497.2 s predicted, a second harmonic of 30.2 ± 1.2 s against 27.5 s. The flat model can copy the delay; it cannot say why the copy has the size and the lopsided shape of light crossing the Earth's orbit, nor why the same number comes out of every century. One term does not fit either model: b₁, 11.6 ± 2.1 s against -1.2 s, a difference between the months before and after opposition, when disappearances and reappearances are timed — the observers' habits, which neither model explains.

## An objection: Rømer's method with modern ephemerides

AlanSpaceAudits (Aether Cosmology Research Group) has published a careful null test of Rømer's method[15]: the times of eclipses of Io, Europa, Ganymede, Callisto, Rhea and Titan over 2023–2025, found from the positions of JPL's Horizons, each fitted with a constant period, and what is left compared with the Earth–moon distance. The slopes do not give the velocity of light back: 460,005 km/s from Io, 116,235 km/s from Europa, a negative value from Callisto. The residuals of Io and Europa keep a period near 436 days, which he reads as tied to the Earth. He writes plainly that he does not claim the Earth acts on Io, nor that c has another value. His question is a fair one: if Rømer's delay is light time, why does the method not return c?

**Our answer is the 437.6-day term of the fit above.** Europa pulls Io into a slightly eccentric orbit whose nearest point to Jupiter turns with their conjunctions (the Laplace resonance[13]). Seen at each eclipse, where the shadow points away from the Sun, Io runs early or late by about 3.4 minutes over 437.6 days — measured here in every era, 199–212 s. Over three years that period sits between the year (365 days) and the synodic cycle (399 days) and cannot be told apart from them, so a fit with a constant period passes part of it into the slope. Europa, in the same resonance, swings far more. With that term in the model, three centuries of eclipses give the light time to 0.7 %, and we think his 436-day peak is the wobble itself; his code is public, and the test is easy to run there.

**Where we agree with him:** Rømer's own observations were few[1], and one to three years of eclipses cannot separate the light time from the satellites' own motions. That is why this section uses 330 years, and the error it gives comes from whole oppositions resampled, not from the scatter of single timings. Read with the IAU's astronomical unit, our A/c means c = 297,589 km/s.

## The analemma: a year of noon Suns, one exposure [evidence: check — Where the radius enters: noon altitudes from an almanac computed for positions on the globe.]

Photograph the Sun at the same clock time every few days for a year and it traces a lopsided figure-8: east–west spread from the **equation of time** (orbital eccentricity + axial tilt, ±16 minutes), north–south from the **declination** swing (±23.44°). Both models below are driven by the same equation-of-time and declination inputs — what differs is the geometry turning them into a sky position. The flat sun's height is tuned *in its favour* so the equinox noon altitude at your latitude is exact.

**Noon Sun at your latitude — measured (any almanac / NOAA calculator)[6] vs the two geometries**

| Date | Measured noon altitude | Globe | Flat (tuned) | Flat error |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Equinox (Mar 20) | **44.9°** | 44.9° | 44.9° | +0.0° |
| June solstice | **68.5°** | 68.5° | 64.4° | −4.1° |
| Dec solstice | **21.6°** | 21.6° | 33.3° | +11.7° |

The "measured" column is what any almanac, sextant, or the NOAA calculator returns — noon solar altitudes are among the oldest routinely tabulated measurements on Earth. The flat sun was granted its best possible height (5.0k km, equinox made exact) and still misses the other dates. Meanwhile its distance to you changes ~1.8× between noon and evening on a June day — so its disc should visibly shrink ~1.8× every afternoon. Measured variation: ±1.7% per YEAR.

> **Two more measured properties the flat sun cannot survive.** **(1) Angular size.** The real Sun's disc varies only ±1.7% over a *year* (perihelion in January — measured with any solar filter or pinhole), exactly matching a mildly elliptical orbit. A local sun a few thousand km up must swell and shrink by a factor of ~2–3 between noon and night, every single day (computed for your latitude in the table note above); it does not. **(2) Sunset.** The Sun crosses the horizon at a sharp, predictable time and is cut by the horizon *bottom first*, at full angular size. A receding lamp would dwindle to a dot near the horizon and never touch it. The analemma, the constancy of the disc, and sunset are three views of the same geometry — and each is a backyard measurement.

## The daily motion: one sky-clock, many observers [evidence: prediction]

What is actually measured, every day, everywhere: the Sun crosses the sky at a **constant angular speed** — 15.0°/h at the equinox, the equal spacing of the discs in any solar time-lapse, the reason sundial hour lines are evenly cut (corrected only by the ±16-min equation of time) — and at **constant angular size**. On the flat disc, a sun circling the pole at a uniform rate cannot produce either: it is close to you at your noon and far at your midnight, so its apparent speed across your sky must surge and slacken, and its disc must swell and shrink. The two panels are driven by the φ slider above; the equinox is used throughout.

$$\frac{d\psi}{dt} \approx {15^\circ/\text{h}}$$
flat, uniform spin: **varying**
flat, tuned for you: **wrong for everyone else**

The flat model's only escape is to make the sun itself accelerate and decelerate along its track so that *your* sky-rate comes out constant. But the sun is one object, shared in real time: the speed schedule that flattens your curve is simultaneously measured by an observer at another latitude — and for them it is worse than the uniform case. There is no schedule that works for two observers at once; the globe needs none, because a rotating sphere gives every observer the same 15°/h for free.

*Figure caption:* **One equinox day, whole-sky chart** (zenith at centre, horizon = outer circle, dots every hour). The globe's path is one arc of a perfect circle about the celestial pole — the same circle long-exposure sun- and star-trail photographs show — entering and leaving at the horizon with its hourly dots evenly spaced. The flat sun traces a lopsided loop whose shape changes with your latitude, with dots crowded on the far side and stretched on the near side — and the loop never touches the horizon.

*Figure caption:* **Apparent angular speed through the day.** Teal: measured (and the globe's prediction) — flat at 15°/h. Amber solid: the flat sun at uniform spin, as seen from your latitude. Amber dashed: what an observer at 10°S measures *at the same moments* if the sun's track speed is re-tuned to flatten **your** curve — the acceleration that fixes your sky breaks theirs. One sun cannot keep two skies uniform; a rotating Earth keeps them all uniform at once.

At φ = +45° the uniform flat sun sweeps your sky at 9–21°/h through the day — a 2.2× swing where clocks, sundials and time-lapses measure a constant 15. Re-time the sun's track to hide that from you, and observer B at 10°S — watching the same sun at the same moments — measures 7–18°/h. The tuning is not free: it is a physical acceleration of the one shared sun, and it must be wrong for everyone it wasn't tuned for. (Mean sun used here; the ±16-min equation of time is the same small correction in both models.)

## The sundial: a stick aimed at the axis [evidence: prediction]

Humanity's oldest clock is the hardware version of the last section. A sundial keeps time all year only if its shadow-caster — the **gnomon** — is set **parallel to Earth's rotation axis**, pointing at the celestial pole. Then the sun simply revolves around the stick at 15°/h, whatever the season, and the hour lines can be engraved once and trusted forever. The price of that trick is written on every dial ever made: the gnomon must lean above the ground by **exactly your latitude** — vertical at the pole, flat on the ground at the equator, 51.5° in London — which is why antique dials are cut for a stated latitude and mis-read when moved. Below, one distant sun held fixed at the left while the planet turns beneath it: drag to orbit, pause the day with the button.

*Figure caption:* **Five dials, one sun.** Seen from the side the gnomons lean at 65°, 35°, 0° and 30° south, each equal to its own latitude. The hour fans below all differ — splayed wide at 65°, tighter at 35°, running *anticlockwise* on the southern dial, and a horizontal dial fails outright at the equator, where the equatorial master dial takes over. Yet every shadow lands on the **same hour**, matching the readout on the globe, computed from one and the same shadow function: the agreement is geometry, not styling.

$${\varphi}$$
$${\tan\theta = \sin\varphi\cdot\tan H}$$
$${15^\circ/\text{h}}$$

All three follow from one fact: an axis-parallel stick sees the sun circle it uniformly. The flat model has no counterpart — under a nearby circling sun, no fixed stick orientation gives season-proof hour lines, the required "tilt" would not follow the simple tilt-equals-latitude law that dials from Egypt to China to Peru all obey, and a dial carried across the disc could not be corrected by latitude alone. Every garden sundial is a standing measurement of the axis it points at.

Watch the planet carry the dials through the fixed sunlight. The shadows sweep *together*, in lock-step, at 15°/h about every gnomon at once; dials crossing the terminator light up — sunrise, on camera — while the far side goes dark. That planet-wide synchrony is what makes "solar time ± longitude" work, and it is exactly what the flat model's surging, latitude-dependent shadow speeds cannot produce.

## The length of the day, measured at forty stations [evidence: measurement]

**The stations of the Baseline Surface Radiation Network record the sunlight every minute,** on instruments calibrated against one world standard, from 79° N to 69° S[8]. Count the minutes in which a horizontal pyranometer reads 5 W/m² or more and you have the length of the day as the instrument sees it. Here are March, June and December 2019 at 43 stations: 116 station-months. In June Ny-Ålesund had 24.0 h of light a day and Syowa, in Antarctica, 0.0 h; in December, 0.0 h and 24.0 h.

$${\cos H_0 = \frac{\sin(-0.833°) - \sin\varphi\sin\delta}{\cos\varphi\cos\delta}}$$
$${d(t) = \sqrt{r_s^2 + r^2 - 2 r_s r \cos\theta(t)} \le \rho}$$

Top line, the globe: H₀ is the hour angle at which the Sun's centre is 0.833° below the horizon (its radius and the refraction), φ the latitude, δ the Sun's declination; the day lasts 2H₀ at 15° an hour. The measured day is a little shorter, 18 min in the root mean square: a Sun just above the horizon does not yet give 5 W/m². Bottom line, the flat model at its best: on the azimuthal-equidistant map the point under the Sun turns around the pole on a circle of radius r, a station sits at r_s, and its day lasts as long as it is within ρ of that point.

> **The flat model, at its best.** Give the spotlight whatever reach suits each month: ρ = 11,029 km in March, 10,675 km in June, 11,367 km in December, fitted to all the stations at once. It still misses the measured days by 3.2 h in the root mean square, because on that map a southern station is far from the pole and the Sun's circle alike: it cannot have long days in December while a northern one has short ones, nor twelve hours everywhere in March. **What would vindicate it:** days of the same length north and south at the solstices, or a spotlight that fits them as well as the globe.

**Limits.** A pyranometer reads 5 W/m² only once the Sun has cleared the horizon by a little, later behind hills, so the measured day is shorter than sunrise to sunset by a few minutes in the tropics and by up to half an hour at high latitude, where the Sun rises slowly; thick cloud can shorten it further. A day counts only when at most an hour of readings is missing, and the stations are those whose data for 2019 are published.

Experiment 6 · the Sun with a pinhole

Measure the Sun's angular size and noon altitude — cardboard, tape measure, stick

A pinhole in foil projects a solar image whose diameter is the projection distance ÷ 107.5 — that ratio *is* the angular size (32′). A vertical stick's shadow at solar noon gives the altitude: alt = atan(stick ÷ shadow). Repeat at different hours and dates.

Independent variable
**What you change:** the **time of day** and the **date** — morning vs noon vs just before sunset; equinox vs solstices. Set latitude above to see the predictions for your home.

Dependent variable
**What you measure:** the Sun's **angular diameter** and its **noon altitude**. The sky decides both.

**Hold constant:** the same pinhole and screen distance; never look at the Sun directly — project onto paper. **The tells, stated in advance:** a distant sun keeps the same 32′ disc from sunrise to sunset (±0.005%, far below your pinhole's precision) and its noon altitude follows the almanac column above; a nearby sun must change apparent size by whole factors through the day and can never reach the horizon at all.

At φ = +45°, June solstice:
globe → noon alt 68.5°, disc 31.5–32.5′ all day, sunset at a sharp time
flat → noon alt 64.4°, disc shrinking ~1.8× by evening, minimum altitude 15.8° — no sunset, ever

**Verdict rule:** if your pinhole shows the disc shrinking through the afternoon and the Sun gliding toward the horizon without reaching it, the near sun is vindicated. If the disc holds 32′ from morning to evening, noon altitudes match the almanac column, and the Sun is cut by the horizon bottom-first at the predicted minute, the distant sun on a rotating globe stands. The two predictions differ by whole factors, not margins.

Experiment 6b · who has daylight right now

Poll one instant across the world — no instrument at all, and the December version cannot be argued with

This is the cheapest experiment on the page: it needs no equipment, no clear sky and no patience. Pick one instant in UTC. Ask people in half a dozen widely separated places whether the Sun is above their horizon at that instant — a message each, or six public webcams, or six live-streamed city squares. Write down six yes/no answers against one clock reading. Both models predict that set of answers in advance, and they do not predict the same one.

Independent variable
**What you change:** the **set of longitudes and latitudes** you poll, and the **date**. Spread the places as widely as you can in longitude; then repeat near a solstice, when the two models diverge most.

Dependent variable
**What you measure:** a single **bit per location** — Sun up, or Sun down — all read at one agreed UTC minute. Not a length, not an angle, not a brightness. Nothing to calibrate and nothing to fudge.

**Hold constant:** the instant. Agree a UTC minute in writing beforehand and have everyone answer for that minute, not "when they get round to it"; an hour of drift is 15° of longitude and will smear the boundary you are trying to find. Take the Sun's *centre* on the horizon as the criterion, and ignore mountains — a valley floor loses several minutes.

**Kit:** none. A group chat, or the webcam pages that Antarctic stations, harbours and ski resorts publish for free. This is the one experiment on this page that a reader with no money, no sky and no daylight can run tonight.

**The tells, stated in advance.** On a sphere the lit region is *exactly half* the surface at every instant, and its boundary is a great circle, so your yes/no answers must sort cleanly on either side of one such line and no other. On the standard flat map — a disc with the north pole at its centre — the lit region is a spot cast by a Sun circling above the tropics, so it must be a compact patch, and places on opposite sides of the disc cannot both be lit.

**Then the December version, which is the one that ends it.** Between late October and mid-February the Sun never sets anywhere inside the Antarctic Circle. On a globe that region is a cap around one pole. On the flat map it is the *entire rim of the disc* — so the flat model must light the whole circumference at once, all 360° of it, while leaving the middle of the disc in darkness. There is no position for a spotlight that does that. Ask two Antarctic stations on opposite sides of the continent — say a Ross Sea station and one on the Atlantic side, some 150° of longitude apart[7] — on any January midnight, and the answers decide between the two geometries without a single number being measured.

**The verdict rule, symmetric.** The globe fails if your polled answers cannot be separated by any great circle — if two places 180° apart in longitude are both in daylight at the same instant while a place between them is dark, or if any Antarctic station reports a genuine night in January. The flat model fails if the lit set is consistently a hemisphere rather than a patch, and if stations spread right around the Antarctic coast all report continuous daylight at once. Both statements are checkable tonight, and only one of them survives.

## Further reading

Not cited above, and no claim on this tab rests on it — listed because it is what you would read next, or because it lets you redo the measurement on data this page did not choose.

- [« Démonstration touchant le mouvement de la lumière trouvé par M. Rœmer », *Journal des Sçavans*, 7 December 1676, pp. 233–236 — digitised by the Bibliothèque nationale de France](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/bpt6k56527v/f234.image). Four pages, in the original French, reporting Rømer's argument to the Académie: the eclipses of Io run late when Earth is receding and early when it is approaching, therefore light takes time. It is the first measurement of a solar-system distance scale that did not depend on the parallax expeditions, and you can read the original rather than an account of it.
- **Edmond Halley, "Methodus singularis quâ Solis Parallaxis sive distantia à Terra…", *Philosophical Transactions* 29 (1716), 454–464.** The proposal that sent the transit expeditions out: observe the transit of Venus from widely separated latitudes, time the contacts, and the difference gives the solar parallax. Halley knew he would be dead before the 1761 transit and wrote it for whoever came after.
- **IMCCE (Observatoire de Paris) and NASA JPL Horizons.** Two independent ephemeris services, one French, one American. Any number in this tab can be regenerated in either, from institutions with no stake in this page's argument.
