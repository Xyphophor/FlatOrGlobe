*Magnetism & the compass · the needle is a 3-D instrument*

*Figure caption:* **How far the needle turns over, along the same 20,000 km.** On the globe the turn completes — nose down, level, nose up. A compass needle is a freely-pivoting magnet: it settles *along* the local magnetic field vector, in three dimensions. The familiar hiking compass hides this by clamping the needle to a horizontal card; free the other axis and the needle **dips** — down in the northern hemisphere, level at the magnetic equator, **up** in the southern hemisphere. That tilt pattern is measured daily, and the two shapes below predict very different versions of it. Drag either scene to orbit it.

That 10° offset is why a compass needs a declination correction at all, and why the correction is different in every place and drifts from decade to decade.

$$\tan I = 2\cdot\tan\lambda$$
$$B = B_0\sqrt{1 + 3\sin^2\lambda}$$
$${B_0 \approx 30\ \text{μT}}$$

The centred-dipole field of a sphere. I = inclination (dip, + downward), λ = magnetic latitude. Two clean consequences: the dip **changes sign** at the magnetic equator, and the total field at the poles is exactly **twice** the equatorial value — 30 → 60 μT, never more, never less. Real Earth adds an ~10° tilt of the magnetic axis and non-dipole wrinkles of ±20% or so; neither ever flips the hemisphere pattern.

## The dip profile: one curve, ten cities, no free parameters left [evidence: prediction]

Both flat layouts have been tuned *in their own favour*, each with the one adjustable quantity it has. The magnet depth (①) is chosen so that its dip-zero circle lands exactly on the observed magnetic equator, ~10,000 km from the pole. Layout ② cannot do that. Its rim ring must be exactly as strong as its centre pole: the two poles of a magnet are always equal, because field lines have no ends (∇·B = 0).[16] Such a layout is a real magnet, not a fiction: a thin disc buried under the plane and magnetised radially outward, with a strength falling as 1/r, carries exactly these two poles, one at its centre and one along its edge.[17] Its field lines leave the edge, arc over the surface, plunge into the centre and run back to the edge inside the disc, so none of them ends. With equal poles, no burial depth brings the dip-zero circle closer than ~13,200 km from the pole, so its depth (~4,100 km) is fitted instead to the ten measured dips below. An earlier version of this tab let the ring be four times stronger than the centre pole, which put the circle at 10,000 km but left the disc with a net magnetic charge that physics forbids; a physicist pointed it out, and it is corrected here. Both layouts are normalised to 30 μT at 10,000 km. Everything else on the curve is now a forced prediction.

> **Where both flat layouts break, in plain words.** **①** A magnet under the centre does produce a dip-zero circle — but beyond it the field dies as 1/r³ and the dip goes *shallow*, not steep: at Melbourne's position the needle should pull ~5× weaker than it measurably does and dip only −19° instead of the measured −69°, and no point on the entire disc can dip past −35° — yet Dunedin measures −72° and one Antarctic point −90°.

> **② The rim-ring version, and what it trades.** Feeding the field back in through a rim ring only trades failures. Its dip-zero circle falls ~13,600 km from the pole, 3,600 km beyond the real magnetic equator, so Singapore and São Paulo get a needle pointing *down* (+13°, +9°) where it measurably points up (−16°, −40°) — a wrong sign, not a wrong size. The far south stays nearly level (Cape Town −1°, Melbourne −9°, against measured −66° and −69°), no point on the disc dips past −69°, and the centre pole makes the Arctic read about 190 μT where instruments find 57.

> **And where the one real pole is.** Measurement instead finds exactly **one** point with I = −90°, off the coast of Adélie Land, Antarctica — claimed overland by David, Mawson and Mackay in 1909 — modern reassessment puts their flag within ~130 km of the wandering pole — and pinned down repeatedly since, most precisely by Barton in 2000, within ~2 km. A point is not a ring, and a working Australian compass is not a spinning one.

> **A limit this page does not hold against either flat layout.** Both draw the field from a permanent magnet buried thousands of kilometres down: ① at about 7,100 km, ② at about 4,100 km. Real rock cannot stay magnetised that deep. Magnetite, the main magnetic mineral of the crust, loses its magnetisation above 580 °C, its Curie temperature, and the rock reaches that temperature on average about 20 km down under the oceans and 20 to 35 km under the continents; deeper rock carries no permanent magnetisation at all.[18] A flat Earth has no stated interior, so both layouts are given the benefit of the doubt and scored on their field alone. But the magnet they need is one no known material can be at that depth.

### So how does the Earth have a magnetic field, if its core is molten?

Because it is molten. Below 2,890 km the Earth's core is liquid, mostly iron: shear waves from earthquakes do not cross it.[19] (The [seismology tab](/ai/seis.md) shows how that was found.) At its base, where it freezes onto the solid inner core, it is close to 6,000 °C,[20] about ten times the Curie temperature of magnetite.[18] It is not a magnet; what it is, is a conductor in motion. The liquid iron moves slowly, and electric currents flowing in it generate the magnetic field, which in turn keeps the currents going: a self-sustaining dynamo.[21] This is the other source of magnetic field that Maxwell's equations allow, currents rather than magnetised matter (∇×B = μ₀J), the same source as in an electromagnet.[16] Computer simulations that solve the equations of this dynamo for a rotating, convecting core produce a field dominated by a dipole, as the Earth's is, which maintains itself for tens of thousands of years and then reverses.[22] The real field has reversed many times: the lavas of the ocean floor froze its direction in, in stripes of alternating polarity on both sides of the mid-ocean ridges.[23] And the process has been run in the laboratory: around the turn of 2000, in Riga and in Karlsruhe, liquid sodium driven through a vessel generated a magnetic field of its own.[24]

## 2,333 measured sites: the dip against every model [evidence: check — Where the radius enters: magnetic latitude from the IGRF pole, a model fitted on the globe.]

The ten cities above carry rounded model values. Here are measurements instead, from two kinds of site. Magnetic observatories record the field around the clock and tie their instruments, week after week, to absolute measurements made by hand with a non-magnetic theodolite;[7] 186 of them published a usable annual mean between 2015 and 2025.[12] Repeat stations are marked points — a pillar, a buried marker — that national survey institutes revisit every few years with the same kind of instrument to keep their magnetic charts up to date; 2,147 repeat and one-off survey stations were measured between 1990 and 2025.[15] Both come from the World Data Centre for Geomagnetism in Edinburgh. Each site counts once, with its latest measurement: 2,333 sites, from magnetic latitude +87° to −88°, 522 of them south of the magnetic equator. For every site the *residual* is the measured I minus the I a model predicts there, with the magnetic axis taken at the year of that measurement. Six models are scored. Two are the globe, both taken as published, nothing adjusted here: the centred dipole of the reference field model, and the off-centre dipole computed from the same model's coefficients by the classical method.[13][14] Four are the flat disc: the two layouts exactly as calibrated above, and the same two with their free parameter fitted to these measurements — magnet depth for ①, burial depth for ② — the best a flat disc of either kind can do on this data. Only the flat layouts are fitted, and on the inclinations only.

> **What would vindicate the flat disc here.** A flat box centred on zero and as narrow as the globe's, with the same median north and south of the magnetic equator. Or, the other way round, a centred-dipole box that sits well off zero, or whose residuals climb steadily from one pole to the other. Either result would count against the sphere; neither needs a large sample to show.

**The method, to redo it from the table at the bottom of this section.** For each site, its magnetic latitude λ_m is computed from the IGRF dipole pole of the year of the measurement; each model then predicts a dip, and the residual is the measured dip minus that prediction.[13] On the flat disc the site stands at the distance r its magnetic latitude would have on the globe, a choice that favours the flat model.

$$\sin\lambda_m = \sin\varphi\sin\varphi_B + \cos\varphi\cos\varphi_B\cos(\lambda - \lambda_B)$$
$$\text{globe: } \tan I = 2\tan\lambda_m \qquad \text{flat ①: } \tan I = \frac{2D^2 - r^2}{3rD},\ r = R\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \lambda_m\right)$$
$$\text{flat ②: } B = \sum_q q\,\frac{x - x_q}{|x - x_q|^3},\quad \Delta I = I_{\text{measured}} - I_{\text{model}}$$

## Why the needle aligns — and how the dip is actually read

$$\tau = \mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{B}$$
$${H = B\cos I}$$

A needle with magnetic moment m feels a torque m×B that is zero only when it lies along the field, north-seeking end pointing the way B points. A baseplate compass pivots about a *vertical* axis only, so it can respond to nothing but the horizontal component H — the dip is still there, expressed as the needle's urge to drag its nose down. That is why manufacturers balance needles differently per hemisphere (counterweighting the south end in the north, and vice versa) and why "global needle" compasses exist at all: an engineering industry quietly built around the very tilt the flat models cannot produce.

*Figure caption:* **A dip circle** (Robert Norman's instrument, 1581): a needle balanced on a *horizontal* axle at the centre of a vertical graduated circle, the whole thing free to turn about a vertical stand. Shown reading I = +64° — roughly Paris.

**Reading it properly (the null trick):**

1. **Level** the base with the bubble — everything is referenced to true horizontal.
2. **Turn about the vertical axis until the needle stands exactly vertical.** In that orientation the needle's plane is *perpendicular* to the magnetic meridian: only the vertical part of B can act on it. No reading is taken — this is a null alignment, insensitive to scale errors.
3. **Rotate exactly 90°.** The circle now lies *in* the magnetic meridian; the needle swings freely under the full field and settles along B. Read I off the circle.
4. **Cancel instrument error:** read both needle ends, reverse the circle face east↔west, re-magnetise the needle reversed, and average — the classical four-fold reversal that removes pivot friction and balance bias.

Before that instrument, dip was a nuisance: compass makers were already gluing tiny counterweights to needles in the 1500s to keep them level. Norman asked *why* the needle wanted to tilt — and measured it.

### The tabletop version: Gilbert’s terrella, 1600

A lodestone machined into a small sphere reproduced the whole dip-against-latitude pattern — and you can repeat it tonight with a sphere magnet and a button compass, then try to match it with any magnet under a flat plate

> William Gilbert machined a lodestone into a small sphere — a *terrella*, "little Earth" — and walked a short compass needle across its surface. The needle stood upright at two opposite points, lay flat along the mid-circle between them, and tilted by the dipole rule everywhere else: the exact dip-vs-latitude pattern navigators were reporting. His conclusion opens the modern subject: *the Earth itself is a great magnet*. You can repeat this tonight with a sphere magnet and a cheap button compass — then try to reproduce the same needle behaviour with any magnet arrangement under a flat plate. The sphere takes one magnet; the plate has no arrangement that works, because the failure is in the shape, not the magnet.

### The escape hatches, pre-empted.

Crustal rocks bend the local field (ore bodies, volcanic terrain) and the whole pattern drifts by ~0.1°/yr — both real, both mapped, and both small: a few degrees and ±20% in |B|, the largest being the South Atlantic weak patch (São Paulo's low 23 μT above). None of it ever flips the sign of I across a hemisphere, moves the dip-zero line to a different circle, or makes a compass fail in Patagonia. Corrections shrink residuals; they never rescue a wrong shape. One honest subtlety. The table's λ is *dipole-frame* magnetic latitude (approximate), not geographic latitude — the axis tilt makes them differ by up to ~10°. Also, the point where a dip needle reads exactly ±90° (the dip pole) sits a few degrees from the ideal dipole axis because of the non-dipole part; that is why the South-magnetic-pole row shows the dipole predicting −81° where the measured value is by definition −90°. A 9° residual against a 60–90° signal, with the correct sign everywhere, versus flat errors of 30–70° with impossible geometry — that is the comparison. The through-line. The other four tabs share one radius R. This tab needs no radius at all: tan I = 2 tan λ and the pole/equator factor of exactly 2 are properties of a dipole centred in a *sphere*, whatever its size. The compass tests the shape alone — and it is the oldest instrument on this page, telling the same story since sailors first noticed their needles leaning in the 1500s.

## Sources and further reading for this tab

The observatory section, the calibration of the flat layouts and the origin of the Earth’s field carry inline [n] markers: the rest are the works this tab was built from, listed in full rather than pinned to individual sentences.

1. [NOAA NCEI — International Geomagnetic Reference Field (IGRF)](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/international-geomagnetic-reference-field) — the standard model behind the "measured" columns; [NOAA's geomagnetic models & calculators](https://www.ngdc.noaa.gov/geomag/models.shtml) let you compute I and |B| for your own coordinates and check every number in the tables.

[British Geological Survey — Magnetic Poles](https://geomag.bgs.ac.uk/education/poles.html) — dip poles vs geomagnetic (dipole) poles, why they differ and move, and the dipole axis tilt of about 10°; the BGS [education pages](https://geomag.bgs.ac.uk/education/home.html) also cover smartphone magnetometers, the sensor Experiment 5 relies on.
3. [Suunto — Understanding balancing zones](https://www.suunto.com/en-us/Support/Compasses-feature-index/Understanding-balancing-zones/) — the manufacturer's own explanation of hemisphere-balanced needles and the global needle: the compass industry engineering built around the dip.
4. [William Gilbert, *De Magnete* (1600), full English translation at Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/33810) — the terrella experiments and the conclusion that the Earth itself is a great magnet.
5. [Australian Antarctic Program](https://www.antarctica.gov.au/magazine/issue-22-2012/science/a-timeline-of-scientific-highlights/) and [National Museum of Australia](https://recollections.nma.gov.au/issues/vol_6_no_1/exhibition_reviews/quest_for_the_south_magnetic_pole) — the 1909 David–Mawson–Mackay journey toward the South Magnetic Pole, the modern reassessment of how close they came, and Barton's 2000 fix within ~2 km of the drifting pole.
6. [ESA Swarm](https://www.esa.int/Applications/Observing_the_Earth/FutureEO/Swarm/Swarm_reveals_growing_weak_spot_in_Earth_s_magnetic_field) and [NASA SVS](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/4840/) — the South Atlantic weak patch: the largest non-dipole anomaly, flagged with * in the tables above.
7. [INTERMAGNET](https://intermagnet.org/), with data served through the [BGS data portal](https://imag-data.bgs.ac.uk/). Around 150 magnetic observatories worldwide, publishing definitive one-minute and one-second data, free to download. **This is the check this tab most needs and has been missing.** Everything in the tables above is read off a model; here are the measurements the models are fitted to. Take the observatories' latitudes, take their inclinations, plot one against the other, and see whether tan I = 2 tan λ falls out of data that nobody assembled to make this argument.
8. [NOAA NCEI / BGS — World Magnetic Model](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/world-magnetic-model). The operational model rather than the research one: the WMM is what runs in the compass app on your phone and in aviation and defence navigation systems, and it is rebuilt every five years because the field moves. A model that had the shape of the Earth wrong would be noticed by its users within weeks.
9. **Robert Norman, *The Newe Attractive* (London, 1581).** The discovery of magnetic dip, by a London compass-maker who found that a needle he had balanced perfectly before magnetising it would afterwards tip downward, and had to trim it to make it lie level again. Nineteen years before Gilbert, and the more concrete of the two: it starts from a workshop annoyance.
10. **Edmond Halley, *A New and Correct Chart Shewing the Variations of the Compass* (1701).** The first isogonic chart, drawn from measurements Halley made himself on two Atlantic voyages. The field's structure has been mapped continuously for over three centuries.
11. [ESA — VirES for Swarm](https://vires.services/). Interactive access to the Swarm satellite magnetic measurements. The same verification as INTERMAGNET, from orbit instead of the ground, and again free.
12. [BGS World Data Centre for Geomagnetism (Edinburgh) — Worldwide Observatory Annual Means](https://geomag.bgs.ac.uk/data_service/data/annual_means.shtml). Annual mean D, H, I, Z and F for some 600 observatories, as supplied by their operators; the observatory section uses each observatory's latest all-days, quiet-days or absolute-observations-only mean from 2015–2025.
13. [IAGA Working Group V-MOD — IGRF-14 coefficients](https://www.ngdc.noaa.gov/IAGA/vmod/coeffs/igrf14coeffs.txt) (NOAA NCEI copy). The Gauss coefficients of the 14th International Geomagnetic Reference Field, 1900–2030. The observatory section takes every globe number from them: the dipole axis and its field strength at the equator (degree 1), and the off-centre dipole (degrees 1 and 2).
14. A. C. Fraser-Smith, “Centered and eccentric geomagnetic dipoles and their poles, 1600–1985”, *Reviews of Geophysics* 25, 1–16 (1987), [doi:10.1029/RG025i001p00001](https://doi.org/10.1029/RG025i001p00001); and M. Koochak & A. C. Fraser-Smith, “An update on the centered and eccentric geomagnetic dipoles and their poles for the years 1980–2015”, *Earth and Space Science* 4, 626–636 (2017), [doi:10.1002/2017EA000280](https://doi.org/10.1002/2017EA000280). The classical method for the off-centre dipole, applied here to the IGRF-14 coefficients.

16. R. P. Feynman, R. B. Leighton & M. Sands, *The Feynman Lectures on Physics*, vol. II, [chapter 13, “Magnetostatics”](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_13.html) (Caltech online edition). 17. R. Fitzpatrick, [“Permanent ferromagnets”](https://farside.ph.utexas.edu/teaching/jk1/Electromagnetism/node60.html), in his graduate lecture notes on classical electromagnetism (University of Texas at Austin); the same treatment in J. D. Jackson, *Classical Electrodynamics*, 3rd ed. (Wiley, 1999), §5.9 C. The field of a permanent magnet follows exactly from effective magnetic charges: −∇·M inside the material and n·M on its surface. A disc magnetised radially with M ∝ 1/r has no charge in its bulk, one pole at its centre and an equal and opposite one along its edge: the flat layout ②.
