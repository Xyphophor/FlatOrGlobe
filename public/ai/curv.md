*Horizon curvature · two different curves*

Two curves are easy to mix up. The **curvature of the Earth** belongs to the ground: it is the same everywhere, one turn per 40,000 km, and it makes the surface fall below a level line by about d²/2R — 7.8 cm at 1 km, 7.85 m at 10 km, 785 m at 100 km. The **curvature of the horizon** belongs to your viewpoint. The horizon is the ring where your sightlines just graze the sphere, and every point of that ring lies at the same angle δ below eye level — the dip.[3] A ring seen from just above its own plane is seen almost edge-on, and a ring seen edge-on looks like a straight line. How curved it looks depends on how high you are, and on how wide a slice of it your eye or your camera takes in.

> **The objection this tab answers.** “The horizon looks perfectly flat from the beach, from mountains, even from airliners — so the Earth is flat.” The globe predicts exactly that. From 2 m, across a 60° field, the horizon of a globe sags by 0.010 % of the frame's width: 0.4 px on a photograph 4,000 pixels wide. From an airliner at 35,000 ft it is 0.724 % of the width, and the one careful study of the question calls it “subtle”: it is not seen in a field narrower than about 60°.[1] A flat-looking horizon at those heights is not evidence against the globe. It is what the globe says you should see.

## In depth the curve is easy to see; left to right it is not [evidence: check — Where the radius enters: distances to the tower read on a map.]

The Earth's curvature shows **in depth**: look along the surface and the far ground falls below your sightline, so distant things lose their base. **Left to right** it hardly shows: the horizon is the ring at the dip δ, and it bends only by δ·a²/2. Both come from the same radius, but the first grows with the square of the distance, the second with the square root of your height.

A documented case shows both at once. On 26 February 2016 Mathias Kp filmed the Turning Torso in Malmö, 190 m tall, from the Danish side of the Øresund: six shots from 25 to 47.9 km with his eye 2 to 4 m above the water, then a seventh from 17.6 m at the last spot.[12] He counted how many of the tower's nine segments the water hid and converted them into metres.[13] At 47.9 km, 114 m of it was gone (the geometric globe says 143 m); climbing to 17.6 m brought 50 m back. From the same 2.1 m, the horizon across a 90° lens bends by 0.7 px on a photograph 4,000 pixels wide. The animation replays his shots, then keeps climbing. From 180 m the whole tower is in view again, and the horizon still bends by only 6.2 px; the bend becomes plain only kilometres up: 48 px from an airliner, 304 px from 400 km.

Every measurement lies below the geometric globe and close to the refracted one. The refraction coefficient that would make the globe fit each shot exactly runs from 0.08 to 0.18 — around the usual 0.13 and as variable as it is measured to be near the sea.[4] The author's own estimates, with a slightly different refraction model, run from 0.076 to 0.187.[13] The plane hides nothing. Over a flat sea the base of a building sits a hair below eye level, at atan(h/d), and a wave hides it only by about the wave's own height — unless the wave, close to the camera, reaches almost to the eye, and then it hides the sea and the horizon as well. The author gives no wave data for that day, only that waves there are usually very small; the eye heights are his own, and a segment read by eye is uncertain by a few metres. Those uncertainties are metres; the hidden parts are 23 to 114 m, they grow with distance and shrink when the eye rises, as d²/2R says.

The same depth effect gives two sunsets. Watch the Sun's top edge vanish, then climb: the horizon drops as the dip grows, and the Sun comes back. Drone pilots film it. One describes the first sunset at about 80 ft above the ground and the second at about 400 ft[10]; another does it over the Atlantic from Ireland with a DJI Phantom 3 SE[11]. Between 80 and 400 ft the geometric dip grows from 9.5′ to 21.3′. On a plane the horizon stays at eye level at every height, so climbing cannot bring back a Sun that has gone below it. These videos give only the pilots' own rough heights: they show the effect, they do not measure it. The [Dip](/ai/dip.md) tab turns the delay between two sunsets into a radius of the Earth.

## Why the curve of the horizon is so small [evidence: prediction]

From a height h the dip is cos δ = R/(R + h), about √(2h/R) — 1.93′·√h with h in metres, the figure every navigator subtracts from a sextant sight.[2][3] Standard refraction bends sightlines along the curve and acts like a larger radius R/(1 − k), with k ≈ 0.13 near the ground[3][4]; it lowers the dip by about 7% (the almanacs, allowing for slightly stronger refraction, use 1.76′·√h[2]), and both values are shown everywhere on this page. Aim a camera at the horizon and take in a half-width a on each side. The middle of the horizon sits at the dip; the two ends sit at the dip too, but they are seen off to the side, and an ordinary lens draws them lower. The gap between the middle and the straight line joining the two ends — the **sagitta**, the measure Lynch used[1] — is, for an ordinary (rectilinear) lens:

$$s = f\,\tan\delta\,(\sec a - 1) \approx \delta\,\frac{a^2}{2}$$

s in pixels (or, divided by f, as an angle at the centre of the frame) · f = focal length in pixels · a = half the horizontal field · exact for a level camera; the page computes every case by projecting the horizon ring point by point

Two small factors multiply. The dip grows only as the square root of the height: 2.7′ (2.5′ refr.) at 2 m, 3.31° (3.09° refr.) at 35,000 ft. And a²/2 is small unless the field is wide: 0.14 for a 60° field, 0.31 for 90°. That is the whole story of the flat-looking horizon. The ground's curvature never changes; what changes with height is how steeply you look down on the ring, and a ring looked down on at 3° still looks nearly straight. Lynch's photograph from 35,000 ft, horizon on the optical axis, 62.7° across, measures a sagitta of **0.51°**[1]; the formula gives 0.57° without refraction and 0.53° with it.

## What a lens does to a horizon

Most photographs “of the curvature of the Earth” show something else, and the reason fits in three sentences. The focal length deforms nothing: changing it multiplies the whole image by a constant, a zoom that only sets the field of view. What changes shapes is the **projection law** — how the angle θ between a direction and the optical axis becomes a distance r from the centre of the image — and the residual distortion of the lens design[5]: an ordinary lens follows r = f·tan θ and keeps every straight line straight; real wide-angles fall a little short of it towards the edges (barrel distortion); fish-eyes follow other laws altogether. And every one of these laws is **radial**: it moves a point along the line joining it to the centre of the image and never changes its angle about the centre. A straight line through the centre is one of those lines, so *every* lens leaves it straight. Anywhere else, only the ordinary law does. That is the whole reason for Lynch's rule — horizon through the centre — and it is what he showed by photographing the sea from 8 ft, effectively sea level, with the horizon near the top, the middle and the bottom of the frame: near the top it arched upward, near the bottom downward.[1]

Start with the 14 mm from 2 m, where the globe's own sag is below half a pixel: the flat horizon arches by 2.85 % of the width near the top and sags by as much near the bottom — more than twice what the globe gives from an airliner, horizon centred, with an ordinary lens over the same 100° field (1.26 %). Switch to the 35 mm law and nothing bends anywhere: the grid stays square, the flat horizon stays straight, and the globe's sag barely depends on where the horizon sits (from 35,000 ft, 62.7° field: 30.3–30.6 px on a 4,000-px frame across tilts of ±20°). The fish-eyes bend everything that is not through the centre, by several percent. Hence the rule for any photograph offered as evidence, either way: an ordinary lens, or a lens whose distortion has been calibrated, and the horizon through the centre of the frame.

Two cases worth working through. An airline pilot photographs the horizon from 35,000 ft with an iPhone 16 Pro at 1× (24 mm equivalent[6], 71.6° across). With the horizon through the centre, the globe predicts a sagitta of 35 px on a 4,032-pixel-wide photo (0.872 % of the width) — measurable, and a little more than Lynch's frame; and since the phone corrects its lens, tilting it moves the horizon without bending it much. For balloon footage shot with an action camera, take the GoPro HERO7. GoPro publishes the horizontal, vertical and diagonal field of each mode, but not the projection law[8]; this page takes the family r = tan(kθ)/k (k = 1 is an ordinary lens, k → 0 the equidistant fish-eye) and fixes k = 0.372 from the three published angles of the Wide 4:3 photo mode. The 16:9 video mode played no part in that fit; the same law predicts its diagonal at 133.9°, against the published 133.6°. From 30 km, horizon centred, the globe's sagitta in Wide 4:3 is 2.69 % of the width; a perfectly flat horizon placed halfway between the centre and the top edge bends by 278 px (6.95 %) — the lens alone. As a payload swings, the horizon travels across the frame and its bend grows, vanishes and reverses; only the frames where it crosses the centre say anything about the Earth.

Read the table with the reports in mind. The numbers say what a camera records, not what an eye notices. A person sees a field much wider than 60°, but judges straightness over a narrower part of it, often through a curved window or glasses, and from above a mile or so the horizon is not the sharp sea line any more but a low-contrast band in the haze.[1] Lynch could not convince himself he saw the curve from Mauna Kea or Haleakalā, although with the same camera the sagitta there is more than half an airliner's (the dip is √(4,205/10,668) ≈ 0.63 of it); the numbers alone do not explain that, and this page does not guess why.[1] This is why the calculator gives heights for a photograph and a measurement, not for a glance.

## The flat model at its best

On an infinite plane the horizon is the vanishing line of the ground: it lies exactly at eye level at every height, and an ordinary lens draws it perfectly straight, wherever it sits in the frame. The plane's sagitta is 0 — not small, zero — from the beach to the stratosphere. That is a clean prediction, and it does not match Lynch's 0.51°. To give the flat model its best chance, let the plane end where the eye stops seeing — a haze edge at some distance D. That edge is a ring around the observer as well, at the depression atan(h/D), and it sags in a photograph by the same formula as the globe's horizon. Tune D so that it matches the globe at 35,000 ft: D = 184 km. Lynch's sagitta is then reproduced. But one D cannot serve two heights: the haze edge's depression grows in proportion to h, the globe's dip as √h. At 2 m that edge would sit 2.2″ below eye level, against 2.7′ (2.5′ refr.) for the globe; from Explorer II, 6.83° against 4.76° (4.44° refr.). The curvature of the horizon in a photograph is the dip spread across the frame — any ring at a depression δ around the observer gives the same sagitta — so the real test is the dip itself and how it grows with height, which the [Dip](/ai/dip.md) tab measures directly. What the sagitta adds is a way to read the dip from a single photograph, without a level.

> **Verdict rule, stated in advance.** *Globe:* a sharp horizon through the centre of an ordinary or calibrated lens sags by the amount the calculator gives — from 35,000 ft across 62.7°, 0.53° with standard refraction and 0.57° without — and the sagitta grows like the dip, as √h. *Plane:* the horizon stays at eye level and straight, sagitta 0.0 at every height. The flat model is vindicated if calibrated photographs from 10 km and higher, horizon on the optical axis, repeatedly show no sagitta to within 2 pixels where the globe predicts a dozen or more. The globe is refuted if the measured sagitta does not follow the dip — for instance if it stays the same from 3 km and from 20 km with the same camera.

**Limits of this tab.** There is one published measurement of this kind, from one flight, and its author placed the horizon on the axis to within a few arcminutes.[1] From high altitude the visible horizon is not the hard edge of the sea: it forms in the atmosphere, where a sightline becomes opaque several kilometres above the surface, and its contrast is less than a tenth of the sea horizon's.[1] The measured 0.51° lies 0.02° under the refracted value; one photograph cannot say whether that comes from the soft edge, the lens or the air. Real lenses keep some distortion even when sold as rectilinear; a sagitta of a few pixels means nothing unless the lens has been calibrated. And refraction near the ground varies with the weather: k = 0.13 is an average, not a constant.[4]
