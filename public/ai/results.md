## One radius, measured 13 ways

Several tabs do more than check that a radius of 6,371 km fits: they run the measurement backwards and let real data give the radius. Each line below is computed on the spot from its tab's data, with the reduction that tab defends.

[measurement] **Measurements alone: R = 6,374 ± 3 km**, from 5 independent methods, 1.2 standard errors from the mean radius of 6,371 km.

[check] **Checks, kept apart:** R = 6,365 ± 153 km from 5 methods, 0.0 standard errors from 6,371 km.

A plane has no radius: the surveying, theodolite and dip methods return an infinite one, and the others find no distances that fit.

### The two results in detail

**Measurements only** (5 of 5 kept): weighted by the inverse of their variances, **R = 6,374 ± 3 km**, 1.2 standard errors from the mean radius of 6,371 km. χ² = 0.8 for 4 degrees of freedom (Birge ratio 0.46). The most precise, Satellite Doppler: receivers' distance from the centre of the orbits, carries 96 % of the weight; one vote per measurement would give 6,385 km. Set aside at more than 3 standard deviations from the others: none.

**Checks**, kept apart because the radius entered their data at some point, even when it was taken out again (5 of 8 counted): R = 6,365 ± 153 km, 0.0 standard errors from 6,371 km; χ² = 4.6 for 4 degrees of freedom. Shown but not counted: An aircraft's weight flying east and west (Eötvös) ; Laser ranges to the Moon from Grasse (station's distance from the centre) ; Doppler shift of the Sun, HARPS-N (local radius at 28.75° N). Set aside: none. This second result is never mixed into the first.

### Lines shown but not counted

An aircraft's weight flying east and west (Eötvös) ; Laser ranges to the Moon from Grasse (station's distance from the centre) ; Doppler shift of the Sun, HARPS-N (local radius at 28.75° N). The Eötvös line because its published data were corrected with the GRS80 radii (see the aviation tab). The lunar laser and the Sun's Doppler shift because each gives the distance of one station from the centre, which on a flattened Earth depends on latitude by up to 21 km, more than their few-km uncertainty.

### How the lines are combined

How each result is combined. Each line's uncertainty adds the statistical error of its median (from the scatter of its values and their number) to a systematic error measured in its own tab: the largest change produced by redoing it with the other reductions that tab presents. An alternative that itself used the radius, such as a wave speed measured with globe distances, is quoted in its tab but never used to size the uncertainty of a measurement. The lines of a group are weighted by the inverse of their variance; when they scatter more than their uncertainties say (Birge ratio above 1), the standard error is multiplied by that ratio, as the Particle Data Group does. A line is set aside when it lies more than three standard deviations from the weighted mean of the others, computed without it.

### Reading the spread

Read the spread honestly. Each method has its own biases, explained in its tab: seismic waves run at different speeds under oceans and continents, the pressure wave's radius scales with a speed taken from the physics of the air, surveyors must correct for refraction, airliners fly detours that make the Earth look bigger, the Eötvös reconstruction undoes a published correction, the lunar laser and the Sun's Doppler shift read a local radius through an ephemeris, and the author's two measurements, one photograph and one filmed sunset, depend on the bending of light on the day and on a few pixels. Al-Bīrūnī's value is quoted from the literature, not recomputed. None of them was tuned to land near 6,371 km, and none of them gives the flat Earth a radius: on a plane the surveying, theodolite and dip methods return an infinite radius, and the others find no distances that fit at all.

### What the orbit measurements do not separate

A yearly swing common to all stars, a yearly Doppler shift and a light time that changes with the season all show the observer moving relative to the stars and the Sun. An Earth at rest under a sky that swings in step every year reproduces the angles exactly (Tycho's arrangement); only the dynamics, why the whole sky would do that, chooses. The rotation measurements have no such twin: a ring laser or a falling ball feels the turning of the ground it stands on.
