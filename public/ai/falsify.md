*The mirror of the front page*

Every tab on this page ends with a number you can go and check. This one collects the other half of each of them: the result that would refute the globe, and the one that would refute the flat model. Not a rhetorical concession — a stated threshold, decided before the measurement, with the precision an instrument has to reach for the answer to mean anything. If one of these results is produced, the model it targets is refuted, and the threshold is written down in advance rather than afterwards.

Writing this table exposed a gap. Nine tabs state what would refute them. Six did not: celestial navigation, horizon dip, gravity, the pendulum, magnetism and GPS carried the measurement and the numbers but never the threshold. Those six rules are written here for the first time, and they still have to be carried back into their own tabs. A page that asks to be checked has to state that line everywhere, not in most places.

Read a row from the middle out. The centre column is what a flat-earth measurement would have to show to end this page; the right column is what a globe measurement would have to show to end the flat model. Both are stated with the same care on purpose — a threshold that only ever fires one way is not a threshold.

## What would not settle anything, and why

**A horizon that looks flat.** At two metres above the sea the horizon is 5 km away and drops 0.04° below eye level — forty times finer than the width of a pencil held at arm's length. Both models predict you see a straight line. A photograph of one is not evidence for either.

**An oval star trail.** As soon as the camera is not aimed at the celestial pole, the image of a circle in the sky is a conic, and a real lens adds its own distortion. Both models produce ovals. The star tab measures angles instead, for exactly this reason.

**A laser that crossed a lake.** Without a documented eye height, a measured distance, a stated time of day and a temperature profile, a laser shot over water is not a measurement of anything. Refraction over a cold surface on a warm afternoon routinely reaches several times the standard coefficient. This cuts both ways: it is also why laser shots are not counted here when they did show curvature.

**Anything an official body says.** Not one row of the table above needs you to trust an agency. Every threshold is reachable with a phone, a stopwatch, a protractor or a pendulum, and the two rows that use published data — earthquake arrival times and the star catalogue — name archives that are open, are maintained by rival institutions in different countries, and would have to be falsified in concert.

> **The one that keeps the page honest.** Any single test can be met with “your instrument was off”, and often fairly. That is why the page's real claim is not any one row: it is that one radius, R ≈ 6,371 km, satisfies every row at once. To refute that you do not need to beat one threshold — you need a flat arrangement that beats two of them with the same parameters. None has ever been shown, and the page would print it if one were.

### Sky & horizon

## Experiment 1 · elevation angles

From the tab [Elevation angles](/ai/elev.md)

Experiment 1 · elevation angles

Two levelled instruments, pointed at each other

Two people stand where they can see each other. Each levels a theodolite with its bubble and measures the zenith angle to the other — the angle down from straight up. Add the two readings together.

Independent variable
**What you change:** the distance **d** between the two stations. Do it at 1 km, then 10 km, then 50 km. You pick these — that is what makes it the IV.

Dependent variable
**What you measure:** the sum of the two zenith angles, **z_A + z_B**. You do not get to choose this — the instruments tell you.

IV d = 10 km
DV flat prediction: z_A + z_B = 180.000000° exactly
DV globe prediction: z_A + z_B = 180.089932°
difference = **5.40′**

**Verdict rule:** if the sum comes out at 180.000° at *every* distance, the flat model survives and the globe is dead. If the excess grows in proportion to d, the globe survives — and dividing d by the excess gives you the Earth's radius. At d = 10 km, a 1′ instrument is enough.

## Experiment 1b · the height sweep

From the tab [Hidden height](/ai/hh.md)

Experiment 1b · the height sweep

One target, one morning, three camera heights

Pick a target across water whose waterline you can identify — a buoy, an anchored boat, a marked pole, a quay. Photograph it from as close to the water as you can stand. Then raise the camera, without moving sideways, and photograph it again. Then again, higher. Count how much of the base reappears at each step.

Independent variable
**What you change:** the eye height **e**. You choose it — a beach, a first-floor window, a hill, a cable car — and you can measure it with a tape or a phone barometer.

Dependent variable
**What you measure:** the height **H** of the target that is cut off at the bottom, read against the known scale on the target itself. The target tells you; you do not get to choose it.

IV e = 2 m, at d = 37.00 km
DV flat prediction: 0.0 m hidden — at every height on this list, for ever
DV globe prediction at your height: 80.1 m hidden (68.1 m with k = 0.13)
the sweep: 0.5 m → 93.3 m · **2 m → 80.1 m** · 25 m → 28.8 m · 100 m → 0.1 m · 515 m → 0.0 m · 1695 m → 0.0 m
climbing the whole ladder uncovers **93.3 m** of the far base

**Verdict rule:** the flat model predicts that climbing changes the target's apparent *size and position* and nothing else — the same fraction of the base is visible from the sand and from 1,700 m, namely all of it. The globe predicts a specific slab returning at each step, set by `d₀ = √(2Re)` alone. If the base of a distant target looks identical from three well-separated heights on the same morning, the sphere is dead. If a measurable slab comes back each time you climb, and the amounts match the row above, the plane is — and R falls out of the numbers.

## Experiment 2 · celestial navigation

From the tab [Cel. nav.](/ai/cnav.md)

Experiment 2 · celestial navigation

Drive north and watch Polaris climb

Measure the altitude of Polaris (or any star) above the horizon. Drive a known distance due north along a straight road. Measure it again. The star has climbed — the question is by how much.

Independent variable
**What you change:** the distance **d** you travel due north. You choose it — 100 km, 300 km, 500 km.

Dependent variable
**What you measure:** the **change in the star's altitude**, in degrees. The sky decides this, not you.

IV d = 300 km north, starting at 45°N. Flat prediction: Polaris rises 1.770°. Globe prediction: Polaris rises 2.698°. They differ by 0.928° (56′).

**Verdict rule:** the globe says the rise is always **2.698°** for 300 km — the same everywhere. The flat model cannot hold that: from 15°N it predicts 0.934°, from 60°N 2.445°.

## Experiment 4 · horizon dip

From the tab [Dip](/ai/dip.md)

Experiment 4 · horizon dip

Climb, and measure how far the sea horizon falls

Stand at the shore with a levelled instrument and measure the angle between true horizontal and the sea horizon. Then climb — a cliff path, a tall building, a hill — and measure again from a known height.

Independent variable
**What you change:** your **eye height h** above sea level. You choose it by where you stand: 2 m, then 50 m, then 300 m. Set it with the height control at the top of this tab.

Dependent variable
**What you measure:** the **dip angle** — how far the horizon sits below level. The world decides this.

IV h = 100 m
DV flat prediction: dip = 0.0′ = 0.000°
DV globe prediction: dip = 18.0′ = 0.299° (with refraction)
geometric, no refraction: 19.3′ · rule of thumb 1.76′×√h = 17.6′

**Verdict rule:** a single non-zero dip, repeated and larger from higher up, refutes the flat model outright — there is no value of any parameter that turns zero into 18.0′. And because the globe predicts dip ∝ √h, plotting your readings against √h should give a straight line whose slope hands you the Earth's radius.

## Experiment 4b · the doubled sunset

From the tab [Dip](/ai/dip.md)

Experiment 4b · the doubled sunset

Watch the same sunset twice, and get the radius of the Earth out of a stopwatch

Sit at the water's edge facing west and watch the Sun's last edge disappear. Then stand up — or run up a dune, or take a lift, or take off — and it comes back. The delay is not an illusion and it is not small: it is δ(h₂) − δ(h₁) divided by the rate at which the Sun sinks. Rawlins proposed this in 1979[3] as a way to measure the planet with a wristwatch and a metre stick, and Minnaert did the six‑metre version by running up a dyke.[2]

Independent variable
**What you change:** your **eye height**, between the first sunset and the second. Sitting to standing is half a metre; a stairwell is ten; an aircraft is eleven thousand.

Dependent variable
**What you measure:** the **delay** in seconds between the two disappearances — one number, from a stopwatch.

Dip at 1.2 m: 1.97′ · at 6.0 m: 4.40′ → Δδ = **2.43′**

Globe predicts a delay of 15.8 s at 52.0° · flat plane predicts 0.0 s

Your 16.0 s, inverted: Rₑff = **7,107 km**, i.e. R = **6,183 km** after removing k = 0.13

**Verdict rule.** The flat prediction is that the second sunset never happens: raise your eye and the Sun stays gone, because the vanishing line does not move. Time a delay of **0 s**, repeatably, from a height difference big enough that the globe predicts 16 s, and the globe prediction is refuted. Conversely a delay that tracks √h₂ − √h₁ across several height pairs, and returns the same R each time, is a measurement no flat arrangement reproduces. Expect scatter: coastal air can shift the dip by minutes of arc, so repeat it and take the open sea over a river mouth.

## Experiment 1f · dawn and twilight

From the tab [Twilight](/ai/twi.md)

Experiment 1f · dawn and twilight

Time first light against the clock, twice a year, from your own doorstep

This one costs nothing and needs no instrument beyond a phone and a clear horizon. Pick a spot with an open view east, go out well before sunrise on a clear morning near an equinox, and write down the clock time at which you can first make out the horizon line and the time the Sun's upper edge appears. Then do it again within a few days of the June solstice from the same spot. The interval is civil dawn, and the calculator below predicts both lengths from your latitude and the date alone, with no fitting and no free parameter. If you are north of about 48.6°, the more dramatic version is available to you: on the June nights the sky never goes properly dark at all, and the almanac will print a dash where the end of astronomical twilight should be.

Independent variable
**What you change:** the **date**, and therefore the Sun's declination. Two dates two months apart are enough at mid-latitudes; equinox against solstice is the sharpest pair. Changing your **latitude** instead works just as well if you are travelling.

Dependent variable
**What you measure:** the **number of minutes** between first light and sunrise. A clock and your own eyes. No angles, no instruments, no trust in anyone else's data.

**Hold constant:** the site, the direction you face, and the criterion you use for "first light" — pick one and stick to it, because the criterion is worth several minutes and the effect you are after is tens of minutes. Clear sky both times; cloud on one morning and not the other ruins the comparison.
**Kit:** a phone. If you want the version with a number in it rather than a judgement call, point the phone's camera east on a tripod, shoot at fixed exposure every thirty seconds, and read the frame brightness afterwards — the curve is the twilight curve and its knee is unmistakable.
**The tell:** at 45° north the interval grows by roughly a quarter between March and June; at the equator it barely moves at all; north of 48.6° astronomical night vanishes outright in June. A flat Sun that dims by distance has no mechanism that makes the fade take longer in one month than another at the same place.

**Civil** 43 min · **Nautical** 61 min · **Astronomical** never ends
Total sunset → −18°: **never ends**. Sun's altitude at local midnight: **−17.70°**.
Astronomical night vanishes above latitude 48.6° · nautical above 54.6° · civil above 60.6°.

**Verdict rule.** At 48.86° with the Sun at 23.44°, the globe says there is **no astronomical night at all** on this date, because the Sun bottoms out at −17.70° and never reaches −18°. Look up the almanac for these coordinates: it should print a dash. If it prints a time — if the sky does go fully dark here on this date — the geometry on this tab is wrong and you should say so.

## Experiment 1f-b · time the Earth's own shadow

From the tab [Twilight](/ai/twi.md)

Experiment 1f-b · time the Earth's own shadow

Turn your back on the sunset and photograph the east — a phone, a wall to rest it on, twenty minutes

Ten minutes after sunset there is a dark blue-grey band along the eastern horizon with a pink one above it: the planet's own shadow, cast on its own air, with the Belt of Venus along its top edge.[6] Rest a phone on a wall, frame the eastern horizon with something vertical of known height in shot — a lamp post, a door frame, a distant mast — and shoot one fixed-exposure frame every minute from sunset until the band has climbed out of frame. Afterwards, convert the band's top edge into an angle using the object of known height and its distance. You will have the shadow's altitude against the clock, and the clock converts to the Sun's depression.

Independent variable
**What you change:** the **time since sunset**, and therefore the Sun's depression θ, which an almanac gives you for your position to the minute.[1] Nothing else moves. If you can repeat it from a very different latitude, or six months later, do — that is where the sharper version lives.

Dependent variable
**What you measure:** the **altitude of the shadow's upper edge**, in degrees, frame by frame. One angle per photograph, read off against a foreground object whose height and distance you know.

**Hold constant:** the camera position, the focal length and the exposure — an automatic exposure will chase the fading sky and move the very edge you are trying to follow. Pick one criterion for the top edge, the colour boundary rather than the brightness one, and keep it. Face the antisolar azimuth, not simply east: ten minutes after sunset the two differ by several degrees.
**Kit:** a phone, a wall, and a foreground object. If you want the angle without trigonometry, hold a ruler at arm's length — a centimetre at 57 cm subtends one degree, near enough for a two-degree effect.
**The tells, stated in advance:** the shadow's top is **never below the antisolar point**, at any moment, which the derivation above gives with no adjustable quantity; it **accelerates**, the ratio in the last column growing from about 1.1 to beyond 5 within twenty minutes; and the sky is fully shadowed at a **depression of three to four and a half degrees** — the same angle in Quito and in Reykjavík, though one takes a quarter of the time the other does.

**What this experiment cannot do, said plainly.** It does not measure R. The single free quantity H is degenerate with the radius here — a bigger planet with a shallower atmosphere gives a similar curve — so anyone claiming a radius from a sunset photograph is claiming too much, and this page will not. Nor is the top edge sharp: air thins gradually, so two careful observers will disagree by something like half a degree, which is a few kilometres of H. What the experiment does settle is *shape*, and shape is what the two models disagree about.
**The verdict rule, symmetric.** The sphere fails if the shadow's top is ever measured at or below the antisolar point, if it climbs at a constant ratio rather than an accelerating one, or if the depression at which it reaches the zenith turns out to depend on where you stand or on the season. The flat model fails if a boundary it must explain as distance and haze instead behaves as a shadow does: an arc curved about the antisolar point, rising at an accelerating angular rate, closing over the sky at one fixed solar depression that is the same for every observer on the planet while the clock time is not.

## Experiment 9b · the Moon against the stars

From the tab [Azimuth](/ai/azim.md)

Experiment 9b · the Moon against the stars

One photograph each, two places, one agreed second — and the sky is the ruler

Agree an instant, to the second, with someone far away from you in latitude. At that instant each of you photographs the Moon with a star close beside it in the same frame. Then measure how far the Moon sits from that star, in arcminutes, in each photograph, and take the difference. That difference is the parallax. You are deliberately *not* measuring a height above a horizon: an angle inside a single frame needs no level, no horizon and no calibration, and refraction lifts the Moon and the star beside it by almost the same amount, so most of it cancels instead of having to be modelled.

Independent variable
**What you change:** your **place**, at a fixed instant. Two observers as far apart in latitude as you can arrange; a few thousand kilometres is already plenty. Without a second observer, the published topocentric position for your own coordinates does the same job rather less convincingly.

Dependent variable
**What you measure:** the Moon's **angular distance from a nearby star**, in arcminutes. One angle inside one photograph. Not an altitude, not a time, not a horizon.

At **21 Dec 2026, 12:00:00 UTC**, **Kongsberg, Norway** and **Charters Towers, Australia** lie 14,445 km apart and both have the Moon above the horizon. Between those two viewpoints the Moon stands **101.6′** apart against the background stars — 3.1 times its own diameter, so a single photograph from each place settles it. That one angle gives **56.98 Earth radii**, against 57.19 from this page's ephemeris, and it needed no distance, no horizon and no clock beyond the agreed instant. The same two sight lines to the **Sun** open by only 17.5″, which is why this experiment does not work on the Sun.

**Verdict rule.** The globe fails if your measured shift does not put d/R within your own uncertainty of the almanac's, or if the answer changes when you turn the baseline. The flat model fails on the table above: the kindest of the three lamp heights at this instant is 3,000 km, and even that one misses some station's altitude by **53.8°** — and at 5 of these 7 places the Moon is below the horizon right now, where a lamp hung over a plane is above it from everywhere, at every height.

## Experiment 1f · gravity and level

From the tab [Gravity](/ai/grav.md)

Experiment 1f · gravity and level

Weigh the same object in two cities, or time a pendulum in both

Every flat model on the table predicts that g is one number, the same everywhere. The globe predicts a specific difference between any two latitudes, set by rotation and the bulge. Two ways to see it: a pendulum, whose period goes as 1/√g, or a good spring‑type balance carrying a heavy mass. Pick your two places, and the calculator tells you in advance how big the effect is and whether your kit can resolve it.

Independent variable
**What you change:** the **latitude** at which you make the measurement. Nothing else — same instrument, same mass, same pendulum, carried between the two places.

Dependent variable
**What you measure:** the **period** of the pendulum, or the **reading** of the balance. Both are proxies for g and the world decides them.

Globe: g = 9.80466 at 43.3° and 9.81124 m/s² at 50.6° → Δg = **657.6 mGal** (0.0671%)

Every flat model: Δg = 0.0 mGal, at both places, exactly

Pendulum of 2.00 m: period 2.83778 s vs 2.83683 s → **0.10 s** apart over 100 swings

10.0 kg on a force-reading balance: **6.7 g** difference

**Verdict rule.** If the two places really do differ, you will see it: 0.10 s over 100 swings is within reach of a phone timing the swings, and 6.7 g is readable on a 0.1 g balance. Experiment 6 · the Sun with a pinhole

Measure the Sun's angular size and noon altitude — cardboard, tape measure, stick

Independent variable
**What you change:** the **time of day** and the **date** — morning vs noon vs just before sunset; equinox vs solstices. Set latitude above to see the predictions for your home.

Dependent variable
**What you measure:** the Sun's **angular diameter** and its **noon altitude**. The sky decides both.

globe → noon alt 68.5°, disc 31.5–32.5′ all day, sunset at a sharp time
flat → noon alt 64.4°, disc shrinking ~1.8× by evening, minimum altitude 15.8° — no sunset, ever

## Experiment 6b · who has daylight right now

From the tab [Sun](/ai/sun.md)

Experiment 6b · who has daylight right now

Poll one instant across the world — no instrument at all, and the December version cannot be argued with

This is the cheapest experiment on the page: it needs no equipment, no clear sky and no patience. Pick one instant in UTC. Ask people in half a dozen widely separated places whether the Sun is above their horizon at that instant — a message each, or six public webcams, or six live-streamed city squares. Write down six yes/no answers against one clock reading. Both models predict that set of answers in advance, and they do not predict the same one.

Independent variable
**What you change:** the **set of longitudes and latitudes** you poll, and the **date**. Spread the places as widely as you can in longitude; then repeat near a solstice, when the two models diverge most.

Dependent variable
**What you measure:** a single **bit per location** — Sun up, or Sun down — all read at one agreed UTC minute. Not a length, not an angle, not a brightness. Nothing to calibrate and nothing to fudge.

**Hold constant:** the instant. Agree a UTC minute in writing beforehand and have everyone answer for that minute, not "when they get round to it"; an hour of drift is 15° of longitude and will smear the boundary you are trying to find. Take the Sun's *centre* on the horizon as the criterion, and ignore mountains — a valley floor loses several minutes.

**Kit:** none. A group chat, or the webcam pages that Antarctic stations, harbours and ski resorts publish for free. This is the one experiment on this page that a reader with no money, no sky and no daylight can run tonight.

**The tells, stated in advance.** On a sphere the lit region is *exactly half* the surface at every instant, and its boundary is a great circle, so your yes/no answers must sort cleanly on either side of one such line and no other. On the standard flat map — a disc with the north pole at its centre — the lit region is a spot cast by a Sun circling above the tropics, so it must be a compact patch, and places on opposite sides of the disc cannot both be lit.

**Then the December version, which is the one that ends it.** Between late October and mid-February the Sun never sets anywhere inside the Antarctic Circle. On a globe that region is a cap around one pole. On the flat map it is the *entire rim of the disc* — so the flat model must light the whole circumference at once, all 360° of it, while leaving the middle of the disc in darkness. There is no position for a spotlight that does that. Ask two Antarctic stations on opposite sides of the continent — say a Ross Sea station and one on the Atlantic side, some 150° of longitude apart[7] — on any January midnight, and the answers decide between the two geometries without a single number being measured.

**The verdict rule, symmetric.** The globe fails if your polled answers cannot be separated by any great circle — if two places 180° apart in longitude are both in daylight at the same instant while a place between them is dark, or if any Antarctic station reports a genuine night in January. The flat model fails if the lit set is consistently a hemisphere rather than a patch, and if stations spread right around the Antarctic coast all report continuous daylight at once. Both statements are checkable tonight, and only one of them survives.

## Experiment 1c · refraction

From the tab [Refraction](/ai/ref.md)

Experiment 1c · refraction

Measure k yourself, and let it convict the model of your choice

Put a total station or theodolite at each end of a line several kilometres long, ideally with a clear reciprocal view. At an agreed instant, each observer measures the zenith angle to the other instrument's centre. Swap the numbers. You now have everything: the sum tells you which geometry you live on, and its excess over 180° tells you the refraction coefficient of the air you measured in.

Independent variable
**What you change:** the **line** — its length s, and the time of day you observe it. Try noon and again an hour after sunset.

Dependent variable
**What you measure:** the two **zenith angles** z_A and z_B, from which the sum and hence k follow with no further assumptions.

Central angle γ = s/R = **0.2698°** (16.19′)
Measured sum z_A + z_B = **180.2347°**
Plane predicts: ≤ 180.0000° — exactly 180 with straight rays, less if light bends down
Globe predicts: 180° + γ(1−k) — an excess that grows with distance
Excess over 180° = **+0.2347°** (+14.08′)
⇒ refraction coefficient **k = 0.130** · R_eff = 7,324 km = 1.150 R

**Sum exceeds 180°.** No flat arrangement can do this: on a plane the verticals are parallel, so straight rays give exactly 180.000° and downward-bending rays give *less*. The excess here is 14.08′, and k = 0.130 sits inside the measured range 0.10–0.34, so nothing exotic is being assumed.

## Experiment 11 · time a star onto a ridge

From the tab [Theodolite](/ai/ct.md)

**Independent variable:** the distance to the peak — the same summit recorded from several places, or several peaks at different ranges. **Dependent:** the second at which the star is cut by the skyline. **Held constant:** one clock discipline (GPS-synchronised), one star, the summit itself as the target rather than a shoulder. **Kit:** a camera that records a timestamp, published coordinates for both ends, and a published altitude for the star[4].

**Verdict rule, announced in advance and symmetric.** The globe fails if the two ends of the same line of sight return the same altitude — difference zero within your error, repeatably, at 9 km and at 131 km alike. The flat model fails if the difference grows in proportion to s, returning one radius near 6,371 km from peaks at three different ranges at once. Either way the refraction setting is stated before the recording and applied the same way at both ends, because a switch worth 15′ cannot be chosen after seeing a 27′ disagreement.

Deliberately left out: any claim about what the recordings show, since none has been checked here; the ellipsoid, which is orders of magnitude below the effect; and terrain models, since the argument is about the angle to a named point, not about a silhouette.
