*Perspective · what it shrinks, and what it cannot do*

“The Sun does not go behind anything: it just gets too far away, by perspective.” “Ships vanish by perspective, and a good zoom brings them back.” “Look at the sunbeams: they spread out from a point, so the Sun is small and close.” All three use the word perspective. This tab shows what perspective is, with nothing but straight lines to the eye, and what it can and cannot do. Perspective makes a distant thing smaller in angle; it never makes the angle zero, never puts a thing above you below your eye level, and a zoom undoes it. And it is exactly what makes parallel sunbeams fan out.

## What perspective is: an angle that shrinks

**The eye does not see sizes; it sees angles.** Euclid put it as a definition: what is seen under a larger angle appears larger, and of two equal things the nearer appears larger[1]. A thing of size s at distance d is seen under the angle θ = 2 arctan(s/2d). Double the distance and the angle halves. However far the thing goes, the angle stays larger than zero: for that, d would have to be infinite. Alberti turned this into a method for painters in 1435: a picture is a window cut through the pyramid of straight lines that join the eye to every point of the scene[2].

*Figure caption:* **Four cubes moving away.** Four 1 m cubes on a square, the lower two on the floor and the upper two from 2 to 3 m, seen by an eye 1.7 m up, in true perspective. They go from 4 m to 2 km, each doubling of the distance taking the same time. The gap between the two rows shrinks with the distance (right: in minutes of arc, both scales logarithmic) and is never zero: 3.4′ at 1 km, 0.34′ at 10 km. The blue frame is a zoom by the ratio of the distances: it shows the same square, gap included, at every distance. The eye level stays between the two rows: the upper cubes never come down to it, the lower ones never rise to it.

$${\theta = 2\arctan\frac{s}{2d}} \approx \frac{s}{d}$$

**The angle seen, never zero.** s is the size of a thing, d its distance, θ the angle it fills in the eye. For d large compared with s, θ ≈ s/d in radians: 1 m at 1 km is 3.4′, at 10 km 0.34′. The curve comes down for ever without reaching zero. What does come is the limit of the eye: about one minute of arc[3], reached by a 1 m gap at 3.4 km. Beyond, the eye no longer separates it; a telescope or a zoom does, because it multiplies every angle by the same factor.

**What this means for a horizon.** On an endless flat floor, the floor's far edge comes ever closer to eye level and never crosses it: every point of the floor is below the eye, so it is seen below eye level, at the angle arctan(h/d). An object standing on the floor is seen below and above that line in the same proportions at every distance. Nothing on a flat floor, however far, can have its base hidden by the floor.

## Parallel lines meet only at infinity

**A pinhole, or an eye, maps a point (X, Y, Z) in front of it to the point (f X/Z, f Y/Z) of its image**, f being the distance from the pinhole to the image. That is all of perspective. Two consequences follow, and the rest of the tab rests on them.

$${x\prime = f\,\frac{X}{Z},\qquad y\prime = f\,\frac{Y}{Z}}$$

**Similar triangles.** A point at height Y and distance Z in front of the pinhole lands on the image at height y′ = f Y/Z: the ray through the pinhole makes the same angle on both sides. The same rule across, x′ = f X/Z. Divide by Z and things shrink as they go away; no Z makes them vanish.

$${\lim_{t\to\infty}\left(f\,\frac{X_0+t\,a}{Z_0+t\,c},\; f\,\frac{Y_0+t\,b}{Z_0+t\,c}\right) = \left(f\,\frac{a}{c},\; f\,\frac{b}{c}\right)}$$

**The vanishing point depends only on the direction.** Follow a straight line of direction (a, b, c) from any starting point (X₀, Y₀, Z₀): its image ends at (f a/c, f b/c), wherever it started. So all lines of one direction, all parallel, run to the same point of the picture: the rails meet at the horizon. Alberti's centric point is the vanishing point of the lines that go straight away from the eye[2]. Two exceptions matter for sunbeams. Lines parallel to the picture (c = 0) never meet: their images stay parallel. And lines going towards the viewer's back (c < 0) run to the opposite point, behind the head, and appear to meet there when the viewer turns round.

## Perspective cannot set the Sun

**What the videos say.** “The Sun circles above the flat Earth and goes out of sight by perspective, the way a plane flying away seems to touch the horizon.” The heights given for that Sun vary, from 3,000 miles (4,828 km)[7] to about 5,500 km[6]; this tab takes 5,000 km, and every result below holds for any height.

**A Sun above a plane is always above eye level.** Its height angle is α = arctan(h/d), positive for every distance d. Even 20,000 km away, half a meridian, the farthest a place on the flat map can be from the point under the Sun, it stands 14.0° above the horizon. To come down to half a degree, the Sun's own width, it would have to be 573 thousand km away, 14 times round the Earth. And it would shrink on the way: from 32′ overhead to 7.8′ at 20,000 km, 4.1 times smaller. The plane flying away is the same case: it seems to touch the horizon only when its angle is too small for the eye, and binoculars lift it back above the horizon.

*Figure caption:* **Two Suns going down.** Left: a Sun 5,000 km above a flat Earth goes from 60° up to 20,000 km away (the side view is at true scale). Under it, what the observer sees looking towards it: it shrinks and stops at 14°, dashed line, the lowest it can be anywhere on the disc. Right: a Sun 150 million km away, the observer carried round by a turning globe; under it, what that observer sees: the Sun keeps its size and goes below the horizon. In both skies the disc is drawn 6 times larger.

$${\alpha = \arctan\frac{h}{d} > 0},\qquad \delta = \frac{D}{\sqrt{h^2+d^2}}$$

**The flat Sun's height and size.** h is the Sun's height above the plane, d the horizontal distance to the point under it, α its height angle above eye level, D its true diameter and δ its apparent diameter. α > 0 for every d and every h > 0: the sign does not depend on the free parameter. With h = 5,000 km, D = 47 km gives the measured 32′ overhead, and δ falls to 7.8′ at 20,000 km. Measured: the Sun's disc is 31.5′ to 32.5′ across through the year[8], the same at noon and at sunset; [refraction](/ai/ref.md) squashes it vertically at the horizon, by about 6′, and leaves its width unchanged[9].

**What would have vindicated the flat Sun, stated in advance:** a Sun whose disc, measured with a pinhole or a filtered camera, shrinks by a factor of a few from noon to evening, and that stops, never touching the horizon, a whole number of degrees up. What is seen is a disc of constant width that touches the horizon and goes under it, bottom first. The [Elevation angles](/ai/elev.md), [Sun](/ai/sun.md) and [Twilight](/ai/twi.md) tabs measure the same thing with numbers.

## Climb a mountain: the sky goes below eye level, but only on a globe

**Perspective keeps everything that is higher than the eye above eye level.** On a plane, the Sun, the Moon and the stars are all far higher than any mountain, so from any summit they are all seen above eye level: from 2 km up, the flat Sun is still at least 14.0° up. And the plane's own horizon stays at eye level: from 2 km up, the edge of the disc 20,000 km away is 21″ below it. On a plane there is no strip of sky below eye level, and nothing to put in it. On a globe the horizon drops as you climb, by δ = arccos(R/(R + h)): 1.02° from 1 km, 1.44° from 2 km. A strip of sky opens below eye level and above the horizon, and in it the Sun after it has set for the valley, and stars that the valley cannot see. A spirit level and a theodolite show it; the [Dip](/ai/dip.md) tab measures the dip, and its [Mauna Loa section](/ai/dip.md#ml-h1) records the Sun still shining after it has gone below the level; [the band below eye level](/ai/dip.md#dp-h1) is worked out there too.

*Figure caption:* **An observer climbs from sea level to 2 km.** Above, the side view (not to scale); below, the western sky at true angles, from 2.2° below eye level to 1.3° above, with eye level as a spirit level gives it (dashed yellow). Left, on a plane: the horizon stays at eye level and nothing of the sky can come down to it. Right, on a globe of radius 6,371 km, after sunset: the horizon sinks below eye level, 1.02° at 1 km, 1.44° at 2 km; the Sun, 0.75° below eye level, shows its top from 227 m and its whole disc from 1,003 m; stars below eye level come out one after the other. Drawn without refraction; with k = 0.13 the dip is 0.95° and 1.34°, and the air lifts objects near the horizon by about half a degree as well.

$${\delta = \arccos\frac{R}{R+h}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{2h}{R}},\qquad \alpha_{\text{flat}} = \arctan\frac{H-h}{d} > 0$$

**The dip of the horizon, and the flat sky's lower bound.** h is the height of the eye, R the Earth's radius, δ the angle between eye level and the horizon on a globe. On a plane, H is the height of a body in the sky, d its horizontal distance and α_flat its angle above eye level: positive as long as H is greater than h, that is for anything higher than the mountain. With R = 6,371 km, δ = 61′ from 1 km; with refraction, R becomes R/(1 − k) and δ = 0.95°. On the plane, δ = arctan(h/d) is 21″ from 2 km to the edge of the disc.

**What would have vindicated the plane, stated in advance:** from a summit, a spirit level whose line runs along the sea horizon, and no star, no Sun, no Moon ever seen below that line. What is seen from mountains, aircraft and the observatory on Mauna Loa is a horizon below the level by the amount δ gives, and the Sun still shining after it has gone below the level.

## Sunbeams: parallel lines, seen in perspective

**Shafts of sunlight through gaps in the clouds, the crepuscular rays, fan out from the Sun.** They are the commonest picture offered as proof of a small, close Sun. But they are the rails of the previous section: parallel lines going away from the viewer, towards the Sun, so their images run to one vanishing point, the Sun itself. Minnaert explained them this way in 1954[4]. The test is to turn round. Side-on, beams parallel to the picture stay parallel. Behind the viewer, the same beams run to the opposite vanishing point, the antisolar point, as far below the horizon as the Sun is above it: they are the anticrepuscular rays, seen and photographed, rarer only because the haze there is fainter[4][5].

*Figure caption:* **One cloud, one Sun, the camera turning.** A cloud layer 2 km up with sixteen gaps; the Sun 15° high. Each gap lets through a shaft of light that reaches the ground 7.5 km further on. The camera, 1.7 m up, holds its lens level (tilting it would make even lines parallel to the picture converge) and turns: facing the Sun, the shafts fan out from it, the “pyramid”; side-on, they are parallel; with its back to the Sun, they close up again towards the antisolar point (cross). Then the view from above, without perspective, and from the side at true scale: all the shafts are parallel, and each patch of light on the ground is its gap moved, not enlarged. Bottom left, the same plan, with what the camera sees.

$${\hat n = \frac{\hat s \times \hat g}{\lvert \hat s \times \hat g \rvert}}$$

**Each beam lies on a great circle through the Sun and the antisolar point.** ŝ is the direction of the Sun, ĝ the direction of a gap, both from the eye. A parallel beam through the gap stays in the plane that contains the eye, the gap and the direction ŝ; n̂ is that plane's normal. Seen from the eye, a plane through the eye is a great circle of the sky, and every such circle passes through ŝ and −ŝ. So every beam is drawn on the sky from the Sun to the antisolar point, and the fan in one direction is the funnel in the other. No distance enters n̂.

## A nearby Sun: the same picture, a different ground

**Take the fan at face value: the best fit.** The usual way to get the Sun's distance out of a photograph of sunbeams is to measure how fast the beams spread and assume the fan lies flat, in the plane of the clouds that make it. Done properly, as a least-squares fit of all 8 beams of the fan in the plane 9.7 km away where their gaps are, it gives a Sun 10.0 km away and 2.6 km up, 590 m above the clouds: a lamp just over the cloud deck, its light falling in a pyramid. The fit is perfect, and it would be just as perfect for any distance: it lands where it is told the fan lies. Such a Sun really sends out diverging beams. And from the eye they look the same, because the previous formula holds for them too: a beam from a point-like Sun through a gap lies in the plane through the eye, the gap and the Sun, whatever the Sun's distance. The fan, the parallel beams side-on and the funnel towards the antisolar point are all there again, on the same lines; only where each beam stops changes. One photograph of sunbeams contains no distance to the Sun: neither close nor far.

*Figure caption:* **The same scene with the best-fit Sun, 10.0 km away and 590 m above the clouds.** The camera turns the same way. Dotted blue: the axes of the distant Sun's beams; the near Sun's shafts lie on the same lines, behind the camera too: seen from above they spread out, yet going away from the eye they still close up towards the antisolar point, as any lines through one point behind the camera do. From above and from the side (true scale) the difference appears: the shafts fall in a pyramid from the Sun, spread out from the point under it, and each patch of light on the ground is its gap enlarged 4.4 times. Where things land on the ground is what tells a near Sun from a far one.

$${\Delta\psi = \frac{b}{D}}$$

**Shadows and light patches on the ground.** b is the distance between two gaps, or two posts, measured across the direction of the Sun, D the distance to the point under the Sun, Δψ the angle between their beams, or between their shadows on the ground. A Sun 10.0 km away makes shadows 1 km apart differ by 5.7°: across a field of posts they fan out, and the patches of light are the gaps enlarged. Seen from above, shadows across a landscape are parallel. That excludes a Sun a few kilometres away, the one a naive reading of the fan suggests. It does not reach far: a Sun 5,000 km up spreads them by 0.011° per km, as the globe tilts them by 0.0090° per km. To tell those two apart takes baselines of hundreds of kilometres, the [Sun](/ai/sun.md) and [Elevation angles](/ai/elev.md) tabs.

**What would have vindicated the close Sun, stated in advance:** shadows of posts or trees, seen from above across a few kilometres, that fan out from a point; patches of light on the ground larger than the gaps above them by a factor that grows with the gap's height; and anticrepuscular rays that do not close up exactly opposite the Sun. What is seen is parallel shadows across any aerial photograph, and anticrepuscular rays converging on the antisolar point[5]. The funnel itself, honestly, proves nothing either way: it is the same for any distance.

## Behind you: a Sun that recedes, a Sun that sets

**The point where the anticrepuscular rays meet is not fixed: it is always opposite the Sun, as far below the horizon as the Sun is above it.** So it moves when the Sun moves, and the two models move the Sun differently. Over a plane the Sun recedes at a constant height: its angle falls towards 14.0° and stops, so the convergence point sinks no higher than 14.0° below the eastern horizon, and the rays always meet in the ground. On a globe the Sun sets: its angle goes to zero and below, so the convergence point rises to the horizon at sunset and then above it. After sunset it stands inside the Earth's shadow, the dark blue-grey band that climbs in the east, under the pink [Belt of Venus](/ai/twi.md#tw-h4); how high that shadow stands is computed in the [Twilight](/ai/twi.md#tw2-h2) tab. Anticrepuscular rays photographed at dusk meet there, above the eastern horizon[4][5].

*Figure caption:* **Looking east, the Sun behind you, both start at 25°.** Left: a Sun 5,000 km above a plane recedes from 10,700 km to 20,000 km away; its angle falls to 14.0° and the rays' meeting point (cross) stays 14.0° or more under the horizon (arrow: below the frame). Right: on a globe the Sun sets, down to 8° below the horizon; the meeting point climbs to the horizon at sunset, then into the sky, in the Earth's shadow under the Belt of Venus. Rays drawn as great circles through the Sun and the antisolar point; the shadow and the belt are drawn schematically (their true heights depend on the air: [Twilight](/ai/twi.md#tw2-h2)).

$${h_A = -\alpha},\qquad \alpha_{\text{flat}} = \arctan\frac{H}{d} \ge \arctan\frac{H}{d_{\max}}$$

**Where the rays meet.** α is the Sun's angle above the horizon and h_A the angle of the antisolar point, where the anticrepuscular rays meet: always −α, from the previous section's great circles. Over a plane, with the Sun at height H and distance d, α never falls below arctan(H/d_max), 14.0° for H = 5,000 km and d_max = 20,000 km: h_A stays at −14.0° or lower, whatever H. On a globe α passes through zero at sunset, and h_A becomes positive: the rays meet above the eastern horizon.

**What would have vindicated the plane, stated in advance:** at dusk, anticrepuscular rays that never meet above the eastern horizon, and no dark band rising there after sunset. What is seen, and photographed every clear evening, is the Earth's shadow rising in the east under the Belt of Venus, with the rays meeting inside it[5]; the [Eclipses](/ai/ecl.md) tab measures the same shadow on the Moon.

## A zoom brings back what perspective shrank, not what the horizon hides

**A zoom multiplies every angle by the same factor N.** What perspective made small, a zoom makes large again, and the cubes' square comes back whole. Perspective hides nothing, it only shrinks. A curved sea is different: the water between the eye and the boat bulges up and hides the boat's lower part, a height H that does not depend on the zoom. A zoom enlarges what is still visible; what is behind the water stays behind it. The [Hidden height](/ai/hh.md) tab measures it on [documented sightlines](/ai/hh.md#hh-tabh) and the [Observation guide](/ai/obs.md) says how to photograph it; [Horizon curve](/ai/curv.md) does the same for the horizon's own curve.

*Figure caption:* **A 20 m boat sailing away, seen from 2 m above the sea.** Above each panel, the naked eye: the boat becomes a dot. Below, a zoom by the distance in kilometres, which keeps the boat the same size. Left, on a flat sea: it comes back whole, sitting just below the horizon. Right, on a globe: beyond 5.0 km the horizon is nearer than the boat and covers its lower part, more as it goes; it is gone at 21.0 km. With standard [refraction](/ai/ref.md) (k = 0.13), the horizon is at 5.4 km and the boat is gone at 22.5 km; the picture is drawn without it.

$${\theta\prime = N\,\theta},\qquad H = \frac{\left(d-\sqrt{2Rh}\right)^2}{2R}$$

**What a zoom changes and what it does not.** N is the zoom factor, θ an angle seen by the eye and θ′ the same angle in the zoomed picture; d is the distance to the boat, h the height of the eye, R the Earth's radius and H the hidden height. N appears only in the first formula. At 15 km, H = 7.8 m without refraction, 6.3 m with k = 0.13 (R replaced by R/(1 − k)); on a plane, H = 0 at every distance and every zoom.

## The usual arguments, one by one

**Each argument, what perspective does, and what is seen.**

| Argument | What perspective does | What is seen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Sun sets by perspective | A Sun above a plane stays above eye level: at least 14.0° at 20,000 km, shrinking 4.1× | A disc of constant width[8] touching the horizon and going under it |
| Ships vanish by perspective | Shrinks them, hides no part; a zoom gives them back whole | The hull goes first and a zoom does not bring it back ([Hidden height](/ai/hh.md)) |
| The horizon always rises to eye level | On a plane, yes, from any height: nothing of the sky can come below it | From 1 km the horizon is 1.02° below the level, with the Sun and stars in between ([Dip](/ai/dip.md)) |
| Anticrepuscular rays meet where they meet | Opposite the Sun: over a plane at −14.0° or lower, in the ground | At dusk they meet above the eastern horizon, in the Earth's shadow ([Belt of Venus](/ai/twi.md#tw-h4)) |
| Sunbeams fan out, so the Sun is close | Parallel beams fan out from their vanishing point, the Sun[4] | Parallel side-on, closing up again at the antisolar point[5] |
| The fan shows where the Sun is | From one place, the fan is the same for a Sun at any distance in that direction | Parallel shadows across a landscape: not a Sun a few km away |

**In one sentence:** perspective divides by the distance, so it shrinks things without limit and never to nothing, keeps everything above the eye above eye level, is undone by a zoom, and makes parallel lines meet at a point that depends only on their direction, which is why sunbeams fan out from the Sun and close up again opposite it.
