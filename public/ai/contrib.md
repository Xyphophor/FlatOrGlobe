## Bass Rock from East Lomond Hill, 40 km across the Firth of Forth [evidence: check — Where the radius enters: the 39.9 km from the camera to Bass Rock, computed from coordinates (the camera placed on the Copernicus elevation model) on a sphere of radius R.]

**The photograph.** [KenRDKAB](https://x.com/KenRDKAB) sent this annotated picture, published here with his permission, taken at 5:28 am on 9 August 2023, just after sunrise, from 345 m up East Lomond Hill in Fife, Scotland. The caption gives Bass Rock as 112 m high and 39.9 km away, and St Abb's Head, on the right, as 75.9 km away. There is no original file and nothing else. The picture shows one thing clearly: the sea horizon passes *behind* Bass Rock, about two thirds of the way up, so the top of the rock stands against the sky although it is more than 230 m below the camera.

**What open data adds.** A first guess puts the camera on East Lomond's 345 m contour, on the Copernicus GLO-30 elevation model[4], where its distances to Bass Rock and to St Abb's Head[8] match the contributor's within 20 m. The photograph corrects it, by parallax. The rock is 40 km away and the coast of St Abb's Head 76 km, so a step of one metre sideways changes the angle between them by 12 µrad. The outline of St Abb's Head is read on the photograph, column by column, and the model's outline, seen over GLO-30 from the camera, is slid along it. From the first guess the model puts St Abb's Head 5.7 mrad (160 px) too far to the right, though once slid the two outlines agree within 0.9 px. Fitted with a free scale, the outline's own shape gives 27,700 px per radian, against 28,158 from the rock's height: the picture's scale is right, the camera stood elsewhere. The angle comes out right only along one line across the eastern flank of East Lomond, and where that line has a clear view of the sea its ground rises no higher than 336 m. The camera is put there, 466 m to the left of the first guess, 1.5 m above the ground: 337 m rather than the contributor's 345 m, at 56.2408° N, 3.2074° W. The two outlines then agree to 0.02 mrad. One thing fits less well: from there the near coast of Fife, at the bottom of the picture, comes out about 1.2 mrad (some 30 px) lower in the model than in the photograph. A camera near 310 m would fit it, but GLO-30 renders the trees and roofs along that coast only roughly, so the height is kept as close to the contributor's as the line allows. From there the rock is 39.5 km away (the contributor wrote 39.9) and St Abb's Head 75.5 km (75.9). Bass Rock rises to 107 m[5]; the contributor wrote 112 m, and GLO-30's 30 m cells round its summit down to 96 m. The photograph has its own estimate: its width there against the angle it spans from the camera gives the scale, and its height in pixels then gives 118 ± 9 m. The Leith tide gauge[6] has a gap from 03:00–05:30 UTC that morning. Four weeks of readings were fitted with 12 tidal constituents and the weather's surge interpolated across the gap; done the same way on every other window of that length, the method is off by 9 cm (root mean square). The sea was −0.02 m from its mean level, so the camera was 337 m above the water. The air comes from the ERA5 reanalysis[1], Open-Meteo's sea temperature[2] and the GFS model's levels up to 750 m[7], at three points along the line of sight.

**Measuring the photograph.** Three edges are read on brightness profiles. The sea horizon, in 50 bands of 20 columns on both sides of the rock, is a straight line to 0.5 px; its slope is the camera's tilt, 0.55°. The rock's outline above it gives the summit, the lowest row of a 5-column running mean. The rock's foot at the water is the median over 100 columns, levelled with the tilt. The horizon cuts the rock at **f = 0.650 ± 0.008** of its height from the foot. That is a ratio of two lengths on the same picture: it needs no scale, no focal length and no time.

$$f = \frac{d}{H}\left[\frac{e}{d} + \frac{c\,d}{2R} - \sqrt{\frac{2ce}{R}}\right]$$
$${c_{\text{globe}} = 1 - k},\qquad c_{\text{plane}} = -k$$

e is the camera above the water, d and H the rock's distance and height, c the apparent curvature: straight light over a surface of radius R/c. The bracket is the horizon's dip below the rock's foot, as an angle, times d. As at Brighton, one picture fixes c and cannot tell the models apart; the photograph needs c = 0.82, so k = +0.18 on the globe and k = −0.82 on the plane.

$$k = 503\,\frac{P}{T^2}\left(0.0343 + \frac{dT}{dz}\right)$$

The refraction coefficient of air whose temperature changes with height at the rate dT/dz (K/m), at pressure P (hPa) and temperature T (K)[3]. In the standard atmosphere, −0.65 K per 100 m, k is about 0.17. Light bends upward (k < 0) only when the temperature falls faster than 3.43 K per 100 m: at that rate the air no longer gets thinner with height.

**What each model needs from the air.** In uniform air the globe's k = +0.18 means −0.6 K per 100 m, close to the standard atmosphere. The plane's k = −0.82 means −16.3 K per 100 m, through the whole 337 m the light crosses. The traced rays say the same with the morning's own layers. Near the sea the air was cold over a warm sea (11.7 °C against 15.5 °C), and above it the GFS model has the temperature almost flat up to 750 m: 0.0 (−0.3 … +0.4) K per 100 m on average. In that air the globe puts the horizon at f = 0.52, 0.58, with a dip of 32,832.2′, a little low. The difference comes from the lowest metres. GFS has the air at 2 m −6.1 K from the sea (ERA5 −3.8 K), and a sea warmer than the air bends the rays that graze it upward, which lowers the horizon. Keep the model's column and change only that difference: with air as warm as the sea the globe gives f = 0.68, and the photograph needs the air −1.0 K from the sea (−1.0 (−1.3 … −0.6)). Those differences come from model cells 25 km wide on a coast, where land cooled by the night can enter, and the wind blew from the land. Put as one gradient over the whole column instead, the same correction looks large, +2.1 (+0.8 … +2.5) K per 100 m or +7 K over the column, because a uniform change of the column is a weak lever on the lowest metres. Given the same freedom, the plane gains little: no air − sea difference alone moves it (f stays > 1.5–> 1.5), and with the column it needs −12.8 (−13.8 … −12.4) K per 100 m over the models' range, still −11.6 with the air − sea difference most favourable to it, −10 K: the camera's air −43 K colder than the sea's, and denser at the top than at the bottom over 337 m. Air like that overturns at once; it can only exist in a thin layer over hot ground.

> **Limits, stated plainly.** There is no original file: the picture is a 1920 × 1080 slide, resized and compressed, the time is the contributor's, the focal length unknown. At dawn the horizon may be a line of haze rather than the sea's true edge; a haze line nearer than the horizon would sit lower and favour neither model. The camera's height (±5 m) moves the globe's f by ±0.02 and the rock's height (±5 m) by ±0.03. The GFS model is a forecast on a 25 km grid, not a sounding, and the traced columns change its whole profile by one uniform gradient, which is only one of the ways the air can vary. The traced globe misses the photograph by 0.07–0.13 in f, about 6–10 pixels: the sea was about 1 K warmer than the air rather than the models' 4 to 6 K, or the air above had a mild inversion, or the other inputs are off by about their uncertainties. The drawing uses the GLO-30 outline of the rock raised to 107 m.

> **The flat model, steel-manned.** It gets the same camera, the same distance, the same rock, the same tide, and it may bend light as it likes. With k = −0.82 it reproduces the photograph exactly, as the animation shows. It cannot do it with straight light, nor with any light that bends down: over a plane, the sea beyond the rock (it runs on to St Abb's Head, 75.5 km away) is seen *above* the rock's summit, which is 230 m below the camera, so the summit would stand against the sea, never against the sky. That is a sign, not a size. **What would vindicate it:** the horizon at or above the summit of the rock from 337 m, with thermometers on the hill and at the shore showing the usual fall of about 0.65 K per 100 m. **What vindicates the globe**, by the same rule: the horizon crossing the rock about half to two thirds up in that ordinary air, as here.

**How to make it decisive.** Keep the original files, for their time and focal length. Photograph the rock from several heights within the hour, from the hilltop down to the shore. On the globe the formula above says where the horizon crosses the rock from each height: lowest on the rock from about 100 m, back up to the summit as the camera nears the water. On the plane, in ordinary air, the horizon stands above the summit from any height above the rock's. A thermometer on the hill and one at the shore give the gradient each model needs before the photograph is looked at.

## Horizon dips from airliner windows: a radius from eleven phone readings [evidence: check — Where the radius enters: the heights (transponder pressure altitudes placed in the GFS model's geopotential heights), the terrain at the horizon, found along each heading on a sphere of radius R, and the ground speeds (GNSS) behind the accelerations.]

**The readings.** [earthlyskeptic](https://x.com/earthlyskeptic) measured the dip of the horizon from airliner windows on two flights with Theodolite, an iPhone app that shows the elevation of its reticle from the phone's motion sensors[9]: the reticle set on the horizon, a screenshot each time, published here with his permission. The flights are AA 6322, Chicago to Des Moines on 5 September 2025 (seven readings: two at cruise over Illinois, five in the descent over Iowa), and AA 5255, Des Moines to Washington on 8 September (four, over West Virginia and Virginia). The dips run from 1.0° to 3.5°, read as they are, with no correction for refraction. Each file also holds the phone's GPS position and altitude, the camera's heading and the time, and the app's reading at the shutter, which can differ from the one on screen by a tenth or two of a degree.

**Where the aircraft was.** Both flights' transponders were logged by FlightAware every 15 to 30 seconds: time, position, course, ground speed and pressure altitude[10]. Matched by the phone's clock, the first flight agrees with the phone's GPS within seconds. On the second, the phone's position had frozen: it is where the aircraft had been 2.5 to 12.6 minutes before. The screenshot labelled 32,173 ft was taken in the descent at 19,825 ft, 6,310 m; the three labelled 15,6xx ft were taken in a holding pattern. Every height here is the transponder's pressure altitude placed in the day's air (the height where the model's air has that pressure[11]). On the first flight it agrees with the phone's GPS within 8–50 m. The heights go from 725 to 8,670 m.

**From a plane, the horizon is the ground.** Over land the line of sight grazes the terrain, not sea level, so the height that counts is the height above the terrain where it grazes, h′. Along each photograph's heading, the Copernicus GLO-90 surface model[12] gives the terrain out to one and a half times the horizon's distance, and the horizon is where the terrain stands highest in angle: 349–409 m under the first flight's horizons, 417–659 m under the second's, over the Appalachians. At low altitude this matters: 350 m of terrain is half the height of the lowest reading.

$$\cos\delta = \frac{R}{R + h\prime},\qquad \delta \approx \sqrt{\frac{2h\prime}{R}}$$
$$\cos\delta = \frac{n_t\,(R + z_t)}{n_e\,(R + h)}$$

δ is the dip, h the aircraft's height, z_t the terrain where the line of sight grazes it, h′ = h − z_t, R the radius. The first line is straight light. In air layered with height, n·r·cos θ keeps the same value all along a ray[13]: the second line is the dip through real air, n_e being the refractive index at the aircraft and n_t at the ground. On an infinite plane the horizon lies at eye level: δ = 0 at every height.

**The real refraction.** No standard coefficient here. The NOAA GFS model gives the air above each aircraft position and above each horizon at the photograph's time, at 21 levels from the ground to 16 km: temperature, humidity and height[11]. The line of sight is traced through that air, which the second formula sums up. The refractive index falls from 258–271 parts per million at the ground to 109–258 at the aircraft. Real air raises the globe's horizon by 0.05 to 0.18°, as a uniform k of 0.12–0.19 would: refraction makes the readings look *deeper* than the globe's, never shallower. The same air over a plane bends a ray from the aircraft back down to the ground whenever it leaves upward at less than a certain angle. An unbounded plane would put its horizon **above** eye level, by 0.8–1.3°.

$$\delta = \delta_{\text{read}} + \frac{a_u}{g}$$

The phone measures its tilt against apparent gravity. An aircraft that speeds up, slows down or turns tilts apparent gravity by a/g, 0.6° for every 0.1 m/s². a_u is the horizontal acceleration along the direction the camera looks, the Coriolis term 2Ω×v included[14]. A plane slowing down, seen forward, makes the horizon read deeper. A turn is a bank, and from a side window most of the bank falls along the view: a bank of 1° moves the reading by nearly 1°.

**The aircraft's accelerations.** They come from the two logged fixes around each photograph, the change of ground speed along the track and of course across it. They tilt the readings by −0.83° to +0.24°, each to about ±0.2° (the log rounds to 1 knot and 1°). The largest is at 3,047 ft, taken while the first flight levelled off and slowed down. Where the log shows a turn between two fixes, the steady side is used if the photograph is within 10 s of it; otherwise the reading would be left out of the corrected fits. A turn's start cannot be placed better than the log's spacing, and a bank of a degree or two can hide in it.

**The surface the readings draw.** As in the dip tab, each reading becomes a sightline from its height on one vertical, tilted by its own dip, and no ground is drawn. Lines that agree on a radius are all tangent to one circle.

*[Figure: Eleven sightlines from heights on one vertical, each tilted down by its measured dip, with no ground drawn; a button shows the circle of 6,371 km and the circle of the radius fitted at face value.]*

*Figure caption:* **Eleven sightlines, no ground.** Each line starts at the reading's height above the terrain of its horizon and is tangent to the circle of the radius its own dip gives. Rule 1: heights are multiplied by the printed factor and the planet is divided by it, at the same scale in both directions, so a line tangent to the true sphere stays tangent to the drawn one. The readings were taken in different places and directions; they are put on one vertical because a dip depends only on the height. The second button subtracts from every reading the constant zero error fitted with the radius (straight light). The lines do not gather on one circle either way: alone, a reading gives a radius between 2,110 and 6,937 km, and between 4,567 and 17,988 km once the offset is subtracted. A single dip read to a tenth of a degree cannot fix a radius; only the trend over the heights can, which is what the fit uses. **Click a line** for its photograph.

**What the readings say.** As they are, with straight light and the true heights, they give R = 4,801 (4,273–5,295) km. That is about a quarter smaller than the surveyed 6,371 km, and smaller still through the day's air (4,309 (3,879–4,708) km). With the phone's GPS heights instead, the frozen 32,173 ft among them, the same fit gives 5,147 (4,780–5,485) km: a wrong height had been making the radius look closer to the truth. Every reading is deeper than the globe's through the day's air, by 0.1° to 1.2°, 0.4° in the median, at low altitude as at cruise. A reading deeper at every height by the same amount is what a zero error does, and removing the accelerations does not remove it. With a constant offset left free, the fit gives R = 5,866 (4,387–7,990) km (day's air, accelerations removed), with an offset of +0.31 (0.00 … +0.65)°. The globe at its surveyed radius then fits with an RMS of 0.29°, the plane's best case with 0.38°.

> **Limits, stated plainly.** The dips are the app's readings, set by eye on a hazy horizon through a cabin window, displayed to a tenth of a degree. The phone's tilt is measured against apparent gravity. The track log gives the aircraft's acceleration only every 15 to 30 s, so a brief bank or a change of speed between two fixes goes unseen, and a bank of 1° moves a side-window reading by nearly 1°. The phone's own zero error is unknown, and the constant offset stands for it. From cruise altitude the horizon may be the top of a cloud or haze layer rather than the ground; that raises it and reduces the dip. The heights rest on the transponder and the model's air, good to a few tens of metres. The terrain comes from a 90 m model along a single bearing, the camera's heading coming from the phone's compass inside a metal cabin. Eleven readings cannot pin down both a radius and an offset tightly, hence the wide range.

> **The flat model, steel-manned.** On a plane with straight light the horizon is at eye level and the dip is zero at every height. In the day's air the plane does worse: its horizon would stand 0.8–1.3° *above* level, the wrong sign. To produce dips of 1 to 3.5° it needs the far ground to vanish in haze at a fixed distance, seen below level, and it may have the same constant offset. Its best case, through the day's air with the accelerations removed, is D = 222 km with an offset of +1.24°. It fits nearly as well as the globe (RMS 0.38° against 0.29°): **these eleven readings alone do not separate a dip growing as √h′ from one growing as h′.** The plane needs 3 times the globe's zero error to do it, and the same haze distance from 700 m and from 8.7 km. **What would vindicate it:** readings with the instrument's zero removed that stay at zero, or grow in proportion to the height. **What vindicates the globe**: the same readings growing as the square root of the height above the horizon, with one radius for all.

**How to make it decisive.** Remove the zero error: take each reading twice, the second with the phone turned half a turn about the line of sight (screen to the window, then screen to the cabin), and keep the mean, as surveyors do with a theodolite's two faces. Read in level, steady cruise, not while climbing, descending, slowing or turning, and note the time to the second: the track log then says what the aircraft was doing. Check that the phone's GPS is live (its position changes from one screenshot to the next). Add readings from a few tens of metres over the sea, where the terrain is not in question and the √h′ curve is steepest. Note whether the horizon is ground, sea or a cloud deck.
