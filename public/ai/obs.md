Every other tab on this page argues from measurements someone else published. This one hands you the instrument. Pick a date and a time, and it draws the Sun or the Moon at its true angular size against a skyline computed two ways from the same terrain — once on a plane, once on a sphere. The two curves separate by a measurable amount at certain azimuths. Go and photograph one of those azimuths at the stated minute, and the photograph decides.

The Sun makes this cheap. Its disc is **32.4′** across, known to a fraction of an arcsecond, so a photograph containing both the disc and a ridge *calibrates itself*: the disc supplies the angular scale inside the frame. No theodolite, no known focal length, no assumption about where eye level was. And azimuth is immune to refraction, which is vertical — so the one prediction the flat map cannot rescue with weather is the one about *direction*.

## The crossing: one point that has to be in two places [evidence: prediction]

The strongest thing at this viewpoint is not a summit. It is the place where two ridges *touch*. Sweep left from Mourre Frey[5] and the near ridge at 16 km sinks; sweep right from Le Grand Mourre and the far ridge at 54 km rises to meet it. At azimuth **241.29°** the two silhouettes cross. Photograph that spot and you have measured nothing — you have only observed that two ridges touch — and yet the flat model is already finished.

> **The two points that meet are on opposite sides of the observer.** The near side of the crossing is ridge **C** in the table, sixteen metres from PeakFinder's point, at **1,683 m** on the IGN's LiDAR — seven metres *below* the viewpoint.[4] The far side, 54 km out, is at **1,809 m**, 119 m *above* it. On a plane only height enters the formula: lower than you is below eye level, higher is above, at every distance. So the two points cannot appear at the same height — and they do.
> On a sphere, at k = 0.13, curvature takes 200 m from the far ridge and 18 m from the near one, and both land near **−5′** (−6′ with straight rays), where the crossing is observed. Put the other way: a plane makes them coincide only for an observer at **1,629 m**, about **60 m** below the real 1,690 m — and no value of k can repair that, since a plane has no curvature to correct.

The cheapest test on the page: no instrument, no clock, no angle, not even a level — two measured altitudes, your own, and a photograph in which two ridges touch. **Its limit, stated plainly:** it works only because the viewpoint sits *between* 1,683 m and 1,809 m, and the lower margin is only **seven metres**. That margin is measured, not inferred — airborne LiDAR is good to about ten centimetres vertically, where the 90 m model used before was off by twenty metres on sharp ground — but two readings shave it. PeakFinder publishes the near point at 1,677 m, where the LiDAR reads 1,683 m at the crossing itself: the published figure is the one not to lean on. And the viewpoint is published at 1,690 m with ground at 1,686.9 m on the LiDAR, so a standing eye at about 1,688.5 m leaves nearer **six metres**. Know your altitude to a few metres — from a LiDAR spot height, not a phone's GPS — or the argument does not close; standing six metres lower would end it. The far margin, 119 m, is not in question.

## Why this viewpoint

The viewpoint is a documented spot at **44.09388° N, 6.92856° E, 1,690 m** in the Alpes-de-Haute-Provence.[1] Its interest is one summit: **Le Grand Mourre, 1,898 m**, 54.1 km away on a bearing of 241.2°. It stands **208 m higher than the observer** — and on a plane, a summit higher than you is above eye level at every distance, because on a plane only height counts. A sphere of R = 6,371 km costs it 231 m of curvature drop over that range, which is more than its 208 m of extra height, so it must sit *below* eye level. Refraction at k = 0.13 lifts it back to roughly level, and no further.

> **A 3°-wide window where the models disagree by 12.7′.** Ray-marching the IGN's LiDAR terrain out to 120 km,[4] the far ridge at 54 km is the skyline from azimuth **238.1°** to **241.2°**. Across that window a plane puts it **7′ to 15′ above** eye level; a globe keeps it within a couple of arcminutes of level (−7′ to +0.5′ with straight rays, −5′ to +2.4′ at k = 0.13). The sign does not flip at every azimuth — the older 90 m model said it did, wrongly — but the 12.7′ gap, 40 % of the solar disc, holds across the window. The Sun rides it on **24 November 2026**: first contact at **16:54:48 CET**, upper limb gone at **16:58:17**, for the standard atmosphere at the viewpoint[6][7]; plausible weather moves those by under ten seconds (the Atmosphere control shows how). Three and a half minutes, the neighbouring evenings nearly as good — one filtered video takes it.

The same kind of dated appointment, at the scale of a continent, is the total eclipse of 2 August 2027: the [Eclipses](/ai/ecl.md) tab states in advance when totality begins at Tarifa and at Luxor, and what a flat model would predict instead.

Two things the panel refuses to do. It does not treat the drawn skyline as evidence: both curves come from a terrain model, which cannot test the geometry that built it — they show *where* to point the camera. And named summits use their published heights, never the model's, so the terrain model never supplies the number a summit test turns on (the LiDAR happens to read Le Grand Mourre, Mourre de Chanier and Mourre Frey within a metre of them[4]). The crossing is the one exception: its two points have no published height good to a few metres, so both come from the LiDAR.

**Verdict rule.** The models disagree about the **sign** here, not the size: above eye level on a plane, below it on a globe. Photograph the summit against the disc. Above → the plane prediction holds and the globe's is refuted. Level or below → no k on a plane can produce that.

> **What would settle it for the plane.** If the ridge photographs 13′ above the Sun's centre at the stated minute — two fifths of a disc high, unmistakable at any zoom — the flat prediction holds and the globe's is refuted. If it photographs level with the Sun or below it, no value of k on a plane can produce that, because a plane has no curvature to correct for. The measurement is the same either way; only the answer differs.

## The arithmetic, in full [evidence: prediction]

Everything on this tab comes out of three formulas, and the argument is entirely about which symbols appear in them. Here they are, with nothing hidden.

$$\tan\alpha = \frac{\Delta h}{d}$$

Δh is the target's height above the observer's eye, d the distance between them. That is the complete formula. Notice what is **absent**: any length to compare d against. Double d and double Δh and α is unchanged, because a plane has no size. This is not a simplification of the flat model — it *is* the flat model, and it has no free parameter left to tune.

$$\tan\alpha = \frac{r_2\cos\gamma - r_1}{r_2\sin\gamma}$$

r₁ = R + h₁ and r₂ = R + h₂ are the two distances from the planet's centre, and γ is the angle the two radii open between them. **γ = d/R is where the whole disagreement lives.** The observer's horizontal is perpendicular to *his own* radius, and the target's vertical has swung away by γ, so the target has to be found lower than a plane would put it — by an amount that depends on **distance alone**. This is the exact form the page computes; it is not expanded or approximated.

$$\alpha \approx \frac{\Delta h}{d} - \frac{d}{2R}$$

Expand the sphere formula and the plane's answer falls out first, followed by a correction of **d/(2R)**. Everything this page argues is contained in that one term. Set R = ∞ and it vanishes and you are back on a plane — so a plane is not a rival model here, it is the **R → ∞ limit** of the same formula. Refraction does not remove the term, it only replaces R with R/(1−k), shrinking it by a factor (1−k). At the standard k = 0.13 that is a 13 % reduction. To cancel it outright you need **k = 1**, which makes the effective radius infinite — that is, k = 1 *is* the plane, and it would take a temperature rising 12.9 °C per hundred metres held over the whole sight line.

A plane, an infinite radius, would need the shoulder at 15.2′, thirteen arcminutes above what was photographed: two fifths of the Sun's disc.

## 24 November, as a measurement: the Grand Mourre with a theodolite [evidence: prediction]

**The 24 November shot is a check, and can only be one.** The ridge's distance and height come from maps, and the Sun's position from an ephemeris that computes it for a latitude on a sphere. To turn the same line of sight into a measurement, the Sun has to be replaced by a level and the maps by a tape. A theodolite reads the angle α between the horizontal and the summit, face left then face right to cancel its index error. The distance s comes from a baseline of a kilometre or so measured on the ground with a wheel or a laser rangefinder, never a GPS, and the two horizontal angles from its ends to the summit. The height difference Δh comes from two recording barometers, one left at the viewpoint and one carried to the summit, with the temperatures at both ends.

$$s = \frac{b\,\sin B}{\sin(A+B)},\qquad \alpha = \frac{\Delta h}{s} - \frac{s\,(1-k)}{2R}$$
$${\frac{R}{1-k} = \frac{s^2}{2\,(\Delta h - s\,\alpha)}}$$

b is the measured baseline, A and B the horizontal angles at its ends. On a plane α = Δh/s, 13.2′ here; on a globe with standard refraction, about 0.5′. Without k, the result is the apparent radius, a bound on R as in the reciprocal-zenith-angle section of the Surveying tab; with a second observer on the summit reading back at the same moment, Δh drops out and the sum of the two angles exceeds 180° by about 25′ on a globe, against 0 or less on a plane.

**What each measurement costs in the result, with the map values used only to plan (54.1 km, 208 m). Planned precisions: α to 10″ (a used 5–20″ theodolite, two faces), s to 0.4 % (a 1 km baseline), Δh to 5 m (two barometers, temperatures logged).**
