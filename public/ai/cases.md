*Case studies · claims examined with their own data*

Each section takes one published observation offered as evidence for a flat Earth, finds where and when it was made, and redoes it with the operators' own numbers and the open data of that day. Both models get the same inputs. The question is not whether the observers saw what they say, but what their own measurements allow anyone to conclude.

## Worthing–Brighton, February 2019: a laser across 15 km of sea [evidence: check — Where the radius enters: the 15.2 km between the piers is computed from their coordinates on a sphere of radius R.]

**The claim.** On the evening of 23 February 2019 a group set up lasers and cameras on the beach at the end of Worthing Pier and at the foot of the West Pier ruins in Brighton, 15.2 km apart across the sea, each about 1 m above the water at low tide.[1] Each side saw the other's laser, blocked and released on command over a livestream; then the green laser at Worthing was lowered to about 0.5 m and 0.15 m above the water, and still seen from a camera 0.7 m up at Brighton. Their presentation concludes that no refraction occurred, since no mirage could be seen, and that no curvature lies between the piers. It also records an earlier attempt, on 2 February, when the laser could not be seen; that failure was put down to a "refraction zone" above the sea.[1]

**Where and when.** The slides give the piers and a map. On OpenStreetMap[7] the laser and camera at Worthing sit on the sand off the south end of the pier, about 50.807° N 0.369° W, and those at Brighton on the beach north-west of the West Pier ruin, about 50.820° N 0.153° W: 15.2 km apart (they give 15.3), all of it over the sea, half a kilometre to two kilometres off the coast. The tide was near its low; the Newhaven gauge[5] confirms the low water just after 20:00 UTC. That evening Shoreham Airport, halfway along the line, reported a clear sky and 3 to 4 knots of wind,[4] and ERA5 gives 1,033.9 hPa.[3]

$$H = \frac{\left(d - \sqrt{2Rh_c}\right)^2}{2R},\qquad R_{\text{eff}} = \frac{R}{1-k} \ge \frac{d^2}{2\left(\sqrt{h_s} + \sqrt{h_c}\right)^2}$$

d is the distance, h_c the camera's height and h_s the laser's above the water, R the Earth's radius. H is the height hidden behind the bulge for straight light: 10.7 m for their first trial, 8.9 m with the usual refraction k = 0.13, so their slides are right that a 1 m laser should be hidden from 1 m. It is seen if the air bends light enough to stretch the effective radius past d²/2(√h_s + √h_c)²: a uniform k of 0.78 for 1 m to 1 m, 0.92 for 0.15 m to 0.7 m. The bulge between the piers is 4.5 m.

**What their thermometers could see.** They read the air at several heights at both ends, with two thermometers good to ±0.25 °C, read to the half degree.[1] The refraction coefficient follows the vertical temperature gradient,[8] and the globe needs only 0.17 °C of warming between the water and 2 m for the first trial (0.21 °C for the lowest). At Worthing both thermometers show the air warming by 1.5 °C over 2 m, 0.77 °C per metre: 9 times what is needed, and at least 0.51 °C per metre within their stated precision. At Brighton the readings are flat between the water and 1.78 m, which their precision cannot tell from any gradient between −0.28 and 0.28 °C per metre, the needed one included. Their own conclusion, that the readings were "indistinguishable within the precision", is exactly why they cannot rule refraction out.

$$k = 503\cdot\frac{P}{T^2}\left(0.0342 + \frac{dT}{dh}\right)$$

P in hPa, T in K, dT/dh in K per metre: with that evening's 1,034 hPa and 8 °C, every 0.1 °C per metre of warming adds 0.66 to k. Air whose temperature does not change with height gives k ≈ 0.22; the standard atmosphere 0.13–0.17.

**Two evenings, two answers.** On 2 February, when the laser was not seen, ERA5 puts the air 2 m above the sea at 2.9 °C over a sea at 9.2 °C, with a wind of 34 km/h off the land.[3][6] Cold air over warmer water bends low rays upward, the hot-road mirage the slides describe; estimated with the usual logarithmic profile of the surface layer, it hides the laser on the globe and on the plane alike. On 23 February the air offshore was as warm as the sea (9.2 against 8.9 °C) under a light wind, and the water at Brighton, 7.0 °C, was no warmer than the air above it: the warm-over-cold layer their Worthing thermometers measured, in which the traced beam reaches the camera on the globe directly. The presentation accepts refraction when the laser disappears and rejects it when the laser appears. Both evenings follow the air; neither tests the shape.

*Figure caption:* The two evenings from ERA5 (air 2 m above the sea, sea cell 50.75° N 0.25° W) and OISST (the sea), 17 to 22 h UTC. 2 February: air 6 °C colder than the sea, wind 34 km/h from the north-north-west; the laser was not seen. 23 February: air as warm as the sea offshore, light wind; the laser was seen. The water at the beaches was colder than OISST's offshore cells: they measured 7.0 °C at Brighton.

**What the night pictures show.** The stills from Worthing show the laser at Brighton just below the lamps of King's Road and well below the British Airways i360, 162 m tall.[1][7] These cannot decide anything: the i360, the lamps and the laser are at the same distance, so the angle between them is the same whatever bends the light, 10.95 mrad between the i360's top light and the laser in straight air, 10.96 mrad with k = 0.78. And with the air it takes to see the laser at all, only about a metre stays hidden on the globe: the lamps 12 to 13 m above the water are seen in both models. "No mirage" proves no more: a steady gradient bends light without doubling any image; a mirage needs the gradient to change sharply with height.[9]

> **The flat model at its best, and the symmetric verdict.** In ordinary air the plane predicts the laser seen every time, from any height; the globe predicts it hidden by about 11 m. That is a clean test, but only if the air is ordinary, and nothing on these evenings showed that it was: on 2 February the laser vanished as it would over a globe in cold air, and on 23 February it appeared in air measured to be warming with height. **What would vindicate the plane:** a narrow beam seen again and again from 1 m at 15 km, by day and by night, while thermometers good to 0.05 °C at 0.25, 0.5, 1 and 2 m, at both ends and on a boat halfway, show the air not warming with height. **What would vindicate the globe:** the same beam hidden, under the same thermometers.

**How to make it decisive.** Their own list of improvements points the right way: measure at the same heights at both ends, and away from the body heat of the people taking the readings. Add the missing pieces: thermometers ten times finer, a reading halfway along the line, and the angle of the laser above or below the true level at the camera, read with a theodolite or a levelled camera. That is where the two models part by a sign: in the air it takes to see the laser, the globe puts it 0.26 mrad below the camera's horizontal, the plane 0.93 mrad above it (0.16 in ordinary air); a good theodolite reads a hundredth of that. Repeat from several heights, as the lasers were lowered here, and by day.

> **Limits.** The thermometer readings are the operators' own, at the beach, at low tide; nobody measured the air over the 15 km between. Each traced verdict applies one end's air to the whole path, which is why the lowest trial, 0.15 m, comes out hidden in Worthing's air for both models while it was seen: the air along the line was not Worthing's. The sites are placed from the slides' map to about 50 m; the distance from their coordinates uses the globe's radius, so the case is a check, not a measurement. The 2 February profile is estimated from ERA5 and OISST, not measured, and the time of that attempt is not given.
