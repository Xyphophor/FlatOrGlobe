## Reverse-engineering the furthest target from two angles

A classic surveyor's trick — the “inaccessible height” — recovers an object's height from two elevation angles taken a known distance apart, without ever reaching it. The reconstruction places the observer and a second station at the distance of the *nearest* example object (both close to eye height, which is why the nearest summit being about as high as the observer matters). Then the angles the **② Cime de Barbin (furthest, 60 km)** *actually* produces are fed back in, and it is reconstructed two ways.

$$H = \frac{b}{\cot\alpha_{\text{far}} - \cot\alpha_{\text{near}}}$$
Law of sines: intersect the two real sightlines in the triangle O–N–T

b = baseline (nearest object's distance), α_far and α_near the elevation angles to the furthest summit from the two stations. The **tangent** method assumes the two “level” lines are parallel — flat‑Earth trigonometry. The **law‑of‑sines** method solves the real triangle of observer O, near station N and target T, allowing for the fact that “level” tilts by b/R between the stations. On a plane the two are the same formula; on a globe they part ways.

## Diagram: what each method assumes [evidence: prediction]

*Figure caption:* **Panels B and C: the same triangle, two reference frames.** The three points O, N and T are the same in both; what differs is the direction each angle is measured from.

Does the globe need a different formula? Not a different *theorem* — only a different reference frame. The sine rule is a statement about *triangles*, not about flat ground: any three points define a plane, and O, N and T are three points, so `a / sin α_far = b / sin(α_near − α_far)` is equally valid in panel B and panel C. What curvature changes is where each angle is measured from — the local horizontal at N is rotated from the one at O by the arc `b/R`, here about 9′ (0.15°).

So the globe procedure is: re-reference the second angle by that rotation, solve the ordinary triangle, then convert the resulting point back to a distance from the Earth's centre. The tangent method cannot do step one at all — parallel levels are baked into `cot`, so it has nowhere to put the `b/R` term. That missing rotation *is* the curvature, and the tables below measure what it costs.

**A caveat worth stating.** With both stations at the same eye height the two sightlines are nearly parallel, so this particular baseline is a poorly‑conditioned way to pin down a height — small angle errors move the intersection a long way. That is a limitation of the *configuration*, not of the geometry: the reconstruction above is computed by intersecting the two true rays in the Earth‑centred frame, which is exact. In real surveying the two stations are deliberately placed at clearly different heights, or the baseline is made long relative to the range, precisely to avoid this. The robust result here is the **angle** comparison in Result 1, which needs no intersection at all.

## Measuring an angle on a sphere

**What curvature is**, a direction that turns with distance, defined without any globe: [Curvature](/ai/kappa.md#ka-h2def).

A common objection: “angles only mean anything on a flat plane — you can't measure elevation on a ball.” But an angle is measured against a *reference direction*, and every point on a sphere has one: the direction a bubble level settles into. That local horizontal is the tangent at your feet, and it exists everywhere. ## Reciprocal zenith angles — the parameter-free test

**The same test run on simulated lines on a plane and on a sphere**, one reading, two, then two hundred: [Curvature](/ai/kappa.md#ka-h2zen).

Point each protractor at the other station and read the **zenith angle** — the angle down from straight up, which is just 90° minus the elevation. Do it from both ends at once. The sum of those two readings is the single most decisive number in this whole page, because the flat prediction contains *no adjustable quantities at all*.

$$z_A + z_B = {180^\circ}$$
$$z_A + z_B = {180^\circ + d/R}$$

If verticals are parallel, the line joining the two stations is a transversal cutting two parallel lines, so the two zenith angles are co‑interior and *must* sum to 180° — a theorem of plane geometry, independent of the distance, of either height, and of how much taller one station is than the other. On a sphere the two verticals meet at the centre, forming a triangle whose angles sum to 180°; the central angle d/R appears as an **excess** over 180°. Measure the excess and you have measured the Earth: `R = d / (z_A + z_B − 180°)`.

**Reciprocal zenith angles between the observer and the furthest target of the current example② Cime de Barbin**

| Quantity | Value | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zenith angle at the observer, z_A | 90.07598° | 90° − elevation (-4.56′) |
| Zenith angle at ② Cime de Barbin, z_B | 90.39316° | the reciprocal reading, 60 km away |
| **Sum z_A + z_B** | **180.46913°** | what the two instruments actually total |
| Flat‑Earth prediction | **180.00000°** | ✗ exact, for any distance and any heights |

| **Earth radius from the excess**, R = d/(sum−180°) | **7,323 km** | inflated by refraction (k=0.13) |

The two readings miss 180° by **28.15′**. That excess is not an error bar — it is `d/R`, the same angle by which the two protractors' 0° lines are tilted in the figure above, and the same `b/R` the flat two‑station method drops in section 04. Note what is *not* in the flat prediction: no distance, no height, no instrument constant. It is 180° or the Earth is not flat.

**Surveyors ran this test on whole networks.** Between 1859 and 1895 the US Coast and Geodetic Survey read both zenith angles of 97 lines in Colorado and California, from 12 to 126 km long: every sum is above 180°, by 6′ to 56′, and the excess grows with the length of the line, as d/R does. The lines, and what they give for refraction, are in the [refraction tab](/ai/ref.md#rf-hsp4).

**How to read these experiment boxes.** An experiment changes one thing and watches what happens to another. The thing **you** deliberately change is the **independent variable (IV)**. The thing you then **measure**, and do not control, is the **dependent variable (DV)**. Everything else must be held constant, or you cannot tell what caused what. A model is testable when it predicts a specific DV for each IV — and it is *refuted* when the measured DV does not match. Both models below make definite predictions, so whichever fails, fails honestly.

[**Experiment 1 · elevation angles** — to do yourself, with every experiment of the site, in “What would refute each model” →](/ai/falsify.md#xp-elev-1)

Why this one is hard to argue with. Most curvature tests can be met with “your instrument was off” or “that's refraction” or “your height was wrong.” This one cannot be met with a wrong height: the heights *cancel*. Put a 5 m station and a 3,000 m station 200 km apart and the sum still comes out at 180° + d/R to the last decimal. The flat model does not predict “about 180°” — it predicts 180.000°, always. Any reproducible excess falsifies it, and the size of that excess hands you R.

Refraction, honestly. Bending reduces the excess by roughly the refraction coefficient, so the observed sum is about 180° + (d/R)(1−k). Reading R straight off an uncorrected excess therefore overestimates it by ~15%, exactly as in the dip method. Geodesists have exploited this since the 1800s: because *simultaneous* reciprocal observations experience nearly the same air, the technique is the standard way to measure k itself — and no value of k brings a curved excess back to zero.

What the demo shows. Rotate it and watch the two 0° tangents: they never become parallel, and their disagreement is the central angle, not an instrument error. This is why a theodolite works perfectly well on a globe — you level it, read an angle against the local horizontal, and get a number. It is also why two such readings taken far apart cannot be combined *as if* their zeros pointed the same way: that, and only that, is the mistake the flat method makes.

## Why you subtract the observer's height

A frequent objection: “why *(h − e)* and not just h? You're smuggling in the globe.” No — this step is derived **inside the flat model, using its own assumptions**. If the Earth is a plane, then every vertical is parallel to every other vertical and the ground is one straight horizontal line. That is exactly what builds a rectangle:

Only the difference of the two heights ever enters the flat formula. That is why this page asks for heights above mean sea level for both observer and target: not to sneak in a curve, but because any constant added to both cancels out. The globe formula, by contrast, genuinely needs the absolute heights, because R + h and R + e are radii from the centre.

### Where tan α = (h − e)/d comes from

Two lines of right-angle trigonometry, and why the choice of datum drops out of them

Read the pair at the top of this tab again and the flat formula is forced, not chosen. The rectangle's far side is e tall, so the part of the target *above* your eye-level line is (h − e): a right triangle with opposite side (h − e) and adjacent side d, so `tan α = (h − e)/d`. Using h alone would measure from the observer's *feet*, not the eye.

And the reference level cancels. Above sea level, the ellipsoid or the valley floor — it makes no difference if both heights use the *same* datum: `(h + c) − (e + c) = h − e`. Only the *difference* enters the flat calculation. The globe formula genuinely needs absolute heights, because R + h and R + e are radii from the centre.

## Angular size: nearly identical, and that's the point

A fair follow‑up: “if the globe changed the geometry, wouldn't mountains look obviously wrong in photos?” No — and the numbers show why. The two models disagree about *where* a peak sits in your view, but almost not at all about *how big* it looks.

$$\beta = \atan\left(\frac{h-e}{d}\right) + \atan\left(\frac{e}{d}\right)$$
$$\beta = \operatorname{elev}(\text{top}) - \operatorname{elev}(\text{base})$$

Angular size β is the angle between the sightline to the base and the sightline to the summit. On the plane it is just the two right‑triangle angles either side of eye level. On the globe both ends are computed with the arctan2 formula and subtracted — the peak leans away from you by θ = d/R, so it is very slightly foreshortened.

**Apparent size versus apparent position, from the current inputs**

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ① Castellaras de Thorenc | 4.5149° | 4.5145° | 1.56″ (0.010%) | 4.16′ = 0.0694° |
| ② Cime de Barbin | 1.4914° | 1.4912° | 0.60″ (0.011%) | 14.08′ = 0.2346° |
| ③ Ubac du Bas Thorenc | 4.0215° | 4.0210° | 1.53″ (0.011%) | 5.14′ = 0.0857° |

The peaks change **size** by at most **0.011%** between the two models — a fraction of an arcsecond, far below what any lens or eye resolves. But they change **position** relative to eye level by up to **14.1′**, hundreds of times larger. That is the whole reason this test works with a level and a protractor rather than a camera: the curve barely distorts what things look like, it moves where they sit. Anyone claiming “the mountains look normal, so it's flat” is checking the quantity the two models agree on.

## How each number is found

What is exact, and what is only drawn. The angle numbers are always computed from the true, un‑exaggerated geometry. The vertical‑exaggeration slider stretches the drawing's vertical axis and nothing else; above 1× the drawn slopes no longer match the printed angles, and a warning on the canvas says so.

Refraction here is a representative average, not a constant. `k ≈ 0.13` is typical daytime air. Real refraction varies with the weather and can even go negative over hot ground, which is a mirage — so the table keeps the un‑refracted figure beside the refracted one, and you can read off exactly how much lift `k` buys.

### How each model computes the angle

Flat plane, globe, and the refracted globe — the three formulas above, written out in words

Flat plane. The ground is a straight line, so this is right-angle trigonometry: with eye height `e`, target height `h` and distance `d`, the elevation is `atan( (h − e) / d )`. A target taller than you is always above eye level; the angle shrinks with distance but never dips below 0°.

Globe. Put the eye at the top of a circle of radius `R` and the target at arc-distance `d` (central angle `θ = d/R`). Against the eye-level line the elevation is `atan2( (R+h)·cosθ − (R+e), (R+h)·sinθ )`. Curvature, about `d² / 2R`, pulls every target below the flat answer; far or low targets fall below eye level, and past the horizon distance `≈ √(2Re)` the base is hidden by the bulge.

Atmospheric refraction. Denser air near the ground bends light gently downward, so distant targets sit a little higher than geometry says. The surveyor's shortcut keeps rays straight and uses an *effective* radius `R_eff = R / (1 − k)`, with `k ≈ 0.13` in typical daytime air (`k ≈ 0.143` gives the “7/6 earth” rule). It raises every globe angle toward the flat value and pushes the horizon out.

Why the picture looks nearly flat. At true scale the drop is a fraction of a percent of the distance, so both panels look almost alike — honestly so. The difference lives in the angles, which a theodolite reads directly.

## Further reading

Not cited above, and no claim on this tab rests on it — listed because it is what you would read next, or because it lets you redo the measurement on data this page did not choose.

- [Bowditch, *The American Practical Navigator*, Vol. 2 (NGA, 2019; PDF hosted by the US Coast Guard)](https://www.dco.uscg.mil/Portals/9/NMC/pdfs/examinations/bowditch_Vol_2_2019.pdf) — the standard reference for sightline geometry over a curved sea: visibility of distant objects, dip and refraction corrections, and the tables navigators actually use.
- [Hirt et al. (2010), *J. Geophys. Res.* — terrestrial refraction coefficient](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2010JD014067) — the k ≈ 0.13 "Gaussian" value used here (Gauss derived it in 1826 from his Hanover survey network), and field measurements showing k varies with weather and can even go negative over hot ground — exactly why this page treats variable-k models as unfalsifiable and keeps k fixed and visible.
- [NGA Maritime Safety Information](https://www.nga.mil/resources/Maritime_Safety_Products_and_Services.html) — the official, free access point for Bowditch and related publications.
- [L. Dettwiller, *Phénomènes de réfraction atmosphérique terrestre*, C. R. Physique **23** (2022) S1, 103–132 (Académie des sciences)](https://comptes-rendus.academie-sciences.fr/physique/item/10.5802/crphys.114.pdf) — derives the refraction coefficient from the vertical temperature gradient and gives κ ≈ 0.17 for the US 1976 standard atmosphere at sea level, the value this page assumes for sightlines close to a water surface. Also the reference for what happens when κ goes large or negative (mirages, hillingar).
- [L. Dettwiller, *Panorama historique de l'étude de la réfraction astronomique*, C. R. Physique **23** (2022) S1, 13–62](https://comptes-rendus.academie-sciences.fr/physique/articles/10.5802/crphys.110/) — notes the long-run working average of about 0.14 for geodesists, whose observations are typically made from relatively high stations on warm sunny afternoons; used here for the Corsica junction, where no coefficient was published for the observations themselves.
- **IGN — Géoportail and the RGE ALTI elevation model.** The French national mapping agency's own coordinates and heights for every summit used on this page. The cases here currently rest on Wikipedia for the observer and target heights; the IGN is the authority those articles are quoting, and it is worth going to it directly. Search "Géoportail" plus the summit name.
- [U.S. Naval Observatory — Astronomical Applications data services](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/data/index). Rise, set and transit times, and altitude/azimuth for any body from any position. If you want to check an elevation angle in this tab against an independent official calculation rather than against this page's own arithmetic, this is where to do it.
- **Marc Bret — *Beyond Range*.** The observer who photographed Pic Gaspard from Pic de Finestrelles in July 2016 at 443 km, then the Pyrenees from the Vosges at 580 km, publishing the predicted panorama beside the photograph each time. First-hand material for the long-distance sightings this tab treats second-hand.
